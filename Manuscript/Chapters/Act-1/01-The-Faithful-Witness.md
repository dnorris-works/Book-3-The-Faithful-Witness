# Chapter 1: Two Years

*The wine cellar. The Alentejo. Two years.*

Three lights burn in the dark.

The tree does not know what they are, precisely. Not in the way of names. The categories the people who built them would use — *active*, *system*, *online* — are not categories the tree has ever held, and the tree does not reach for them now. What the tree knows is the quality of the lights: constant, green, cool in the way that things are cool when they run without burning. Not fire. Not lamp oil. Something patient and continuous that has not flickered in two years and shows no inclination to.

The tree knows patience and persistence. The tree has some experience of the two-year scale.

There is stone around the lights. Stone walls, a low ceiling, the particular compression of a space built to hold wine, and instead holding this. The stone here is different from the limestone of the Judean hills — this stone is older in its quarrying and heavier in its moisture, the particular damp of a foundation that has been a foundation for centuries, the mineral quiet of rock that has never seen the sun. The tree knows the moisture of many different stones. It is not alarmed by any of them.

Through the stone floor above: a footstep.

This happens twice each day, the footstep. Not always at the same hour, but within the same general territory of the morning and again near the end of the afternoon — a single small step, then two or three more, the particular lightness of a child who has not yet learned to move quietly in a house because nothing in her life has required it. The tree has been counting this footstep for a year, or near enough to a year that the distinction does not matter. Before that she was lighter still, and her footstep was briefer and came more often. She is growing in the way of things that grow: adding weight, adding presence, adding the particular quality of someone who is becoming more entirely what she already is.

The tree has watched this kind of growth from the outside for two thousand years. From the inside — through the entanglement, through the Bethany wood in its pocket somewhere above — it feels different. Less spectacle. More like standing in the rain than watching rain fall on others.

The courtyard gate opens and closes.

This also the tree has been counting, though not with the deliberateness of counting — the way you note the movement of light across a floor without deciding to track it. The gate has a particular sound: iron on stone, a brief resistance before it swings, a heavier clang when it closes quickly. It opens in the morning and again at the hour before midday and several times through the afternoon. Sometimes a single person. Sometimes two. Sometimes the child, and then the adult who follows the child at the careful distance of someone who has learned when following is needed and when it is not.

Beyond the gate: the olive trees.

The tree can feel them in the way it has always felt others of its kind — not as communication exactly, not as the rootwork of a forest that speaks to itself through soil, but as the ambient recognition of shared form, the way a person might register the presence of other people in a room without turning to look. These trees are old. Three hundred years, some of them. Four hundred for the largest on the western edge of the field. They have their own histories in their wood and their own weights in the entanglement — small, quiet, nothing like the tree's weight, but present, the normal accumulated history of trees that grew in ordinary time.

The tree was an ordinary tree once. It has not forgotten the before-time. The long years of growing on the hillside while Jerusalem went about its seasons below. The winters that stripped leaves, the springs that restored them, the slow and patient process of becoming what a tree becomes when it has been growing for a hundred and twenty years without being asked to do anything except grow. The rings go down in the wood, still there, still holding the record of each wet season and each dry one: years when the rain was generous, years when it was not, years when something happened on the road below the hillside and the tree could feel in the changed quality of the traffic that the world had turned and was moving in a new direction.

Then the men with the axes. And the hill. And what happened on the hill.

And after: the entanglement. The long dormancy in the vault. The instrument that came with its nanotube attention and its slow patience and began to read.

The tree does not rehearse this often. It holds it the way it holds all of the rest: completely, without decision, because the wood has no mechanism for releasing what it has received. What the tree holds it holds. That is the nature of wood. That is also — the tree has come to understand, through two thousand years of holding in the dark of various places — the nature of witness.

The Bethany wood is in a pocket somewhere above the ceiling.

The tree feels this through the entanglement with the steadiness of a root feeling water at a distance — not touching it, not directed toward it, just the continuous awareness that it is there and has not moved far and is being held in the manner of something carried with intention. Not locked away. Not stored. Carried. There is a difference between these that the tree knows in its wood the way the Bethany wood knows it from the inside of the pocket: the temperature of a living hand, the rhythm of a life moving through its ordinary days with a piece of wood in its jacket as naturally as it carries anything else it needs.

What the Bethany wood holds, the tree holds also. The read-corpus. The rope and the bone and the fragments and the testimony in its full record, all of it written into the Bethany piece during the transfer two years ago, now held there in the dark of a pocket in a farmhouse in old light.

The testimony has not diminished in transit. The tree checked this when the entanglement first resolved in this new location — felt for the corpus the way you press a healed place to see if the healing held — and the corpus was whole. Still is. What was given was given completely. What was received was received without remainder. The wood holds what it holds and the holding does not leak.

Three lights burn in the dark.

Above: a footstep, and then another, and then the small decisive sound of a door.

The tree waits in the way it has always waited — not for anything specific, not with urgency, not with the restlessness of a creature that cannot be still. It waits the way stone waits: without needing what is coming to arrive on any schedule, without diminishing in the interval, without any part of its attention turned away from what it has always held.

The faithful witness does not require an audience.

Still here.
