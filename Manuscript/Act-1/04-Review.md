# Chapter 4: Review

*The farmhouse. The Alentejo. Afternoon.*

He heard the school bus before he saw it, the particular sound of a diesel engine on the lane, unhurried, making its stop-and-start progress through the village outskirts before the turn that brought it past the farmhouse gate. Daniel had been at the kitchen table with a book he was not really reading, and he set it down and waited.

The gate latch. Footsteps on the gravel: two sets, then one, the bus pulling away, and then Jessie appeared in the kitchen doorway with her bag on her back and Gerald under her arm and a look on her face that he had learned to recognize, the specific expression of someone who has been carrying a piece of information since morning and is finally in a position to deliver it.

"Leonardo moved," she said.

"I know. I saw him this morning."

Jessie set Gerald carefully on the kitchen table and unslung her bag. "You saw him move or you saw where he moved to?"

"Where he moved to. Two stones down."

She pulled out a workbook and set it beside Gerald without looking at it. "That's not the whole situation."

Daniel waited.

"He was also on the east side of the wall this afternoon." She climbed onto the chair across from Gerald and arranged herself. "Inês saw him when the bus went past. She said he was in a completely different position."

Inês was a girl from school whose observations Jessie considered reliable, though her analytical framework was apparently not always up to the task of interpreting them correctly. Daniel had gathered this from previous briefings.

"So he moved twice today."

"At least twice." Jessie folded her hands on the table in the manner of someone convening a formal proceeding. "Gerald and I have been discussing this. Gerald believes there is a pattern."

"What kind of pattern?"

"Expansion." She said it with the weight it deserved. "Gerald thinks Leonardo is not making a statement about the portfolio review. Gerald thinks Leonardo is conducting an assessment."

"Of what?"

Jessie looked at him with the patient expression of someone addressing a question that should not require asking. "The territory."

Daniel considered this. Outside the kitchen window, the courtyard was quiet in the afternoon light, the low wall where Leonardo had spent most of the past two years now conspicuously empty. He had not thought of it as an expansion before. He thought of it as now.

"What does the governance framework say about territorial assessments?"

"That's what the review is for." Jessie picked up her workbook and opened it to a page that had nothing to do with lizard governance, then closed it again. She did a fair amount of this, opening and closing things while she was thinking. "The current framework only covers the wall. There's nothing about the courtyard."

"Is the courtyard in play?"

"Gerald thinks the gate area might be in play." A pause. "And possibly the fig tree root."

Daniel looked at his daughter across the kitchen table. She was still in her school clothes, a small smear of something on her sleeve that was probably paint, her Portuguese braids slightly looser than they had been that morning. Gerald sat opposite her, composed as always, awaiting the outcome of the deliberations with his characteristic equanimity.

"You're going to need new terms of reference," Daniel said.

"Yes." Jessie nodded slowly, the nod of someone who has already arrived at this conclusion but wanted it confirmed. "Gerald says the review has to happen before Friday."

"Why Friday?"

"Because after Friday it becomes a precedent." She said this in Portuguese, *um precedente*, and then switched back to English without noticing she'd done it. "If he's in the new territory for a week and there's no governance response, the framework automatically accommodates the expansion."

Daniel did not know whether this was a rule Jessie had invented or something she had absorbed from school, where the teacher apparently ran a classroom sufficiently structured that concepts like precedent had found their way into Jessie's working vocabulary. He had decided some weeks ago that it did not matter. The framework was internally consistent. That was the important thing.

"I think you should conduct a site inspection first," he said. "Before you write anything. Go and see where he is."

Jessie looked at the courtyard window. "He might not be there now."

"Then you document the absence."

She considered this. "Can I take Gerald?"

"Gerald should probably be there. For the record."

This satisfied her. She gathered Gerald and her bag and went out through the kitchen door to the courtyard, and Daniel heard the soft sound of the gate latch, and then her voice in Portuguese, not talking to him, not talking to Gerald exactly, more the low running commentary she kept up when she was thinking through something, the way some people read aloud to themselves. He could hear the shape of the sentences without the words, the rhythm of a six-year-old working through a problem.

He got up to start dinner.

He was cutting vegetables when she came back in, Gerald repositioned under her arm with the gravity of a bear who has participated in a significant inspection.

"He's not there," she said. "But his traces are."

"What traces?"

"Disturbance in the dirt near the root." She set Gerald on the table and climbed up again. "Gerald documented it."

"Good." Daniel slid the vegetables into the pan. "Then you have evidence."

Jessie was quiet for a moment, watching him cook. This sometimes happened after school: the governance business dispatched, she would deflate slightly into a different, quieter version of herself, the operational mode giving way to something more ordinary. She reached across and adjusted Gerald's position without apparent purpose.

"Papá prays before dinner," she said.

He did not stop what he was doing. He turned the heat down on the pan and stirred and did not look at her.

"Yes," he said. "He does."

"Mamã doesn't."

"No."

Jessie seemed to be considering how to file this. Not asking for an explanation: she was not asking in the way she asked governance questions, with a specific answer required. She was doing what she did with things that were adjacent to the rules without being covered by them, noting them, sitting with them, leaving them open.

"Inês's family all prays," she said. "Inês prays too. She said she prays for her cat."

"That's a good thing to pray for."

"The cat is fine," Jessie said, a small note of accuracy entering her voice. "Inês just likes praying for him."

"Still a good thing."

Jessie reached out and moved Gerald a centimeter to the left for no reason Daniel could identify. She looked at the courtyard window. She looked at Gerald.

"Does it matter?" she said. "The difference."

Daniel was quiet for a moment. He did not manufacture anything. He let the question sit there, which was the right thing to do with a question like that.

"I don't know yet," he said. "I'm still thinking about it."

Jessie received this. She did not press further, which was one of the things about her that he found himself noting often without writing down: this particular quality of knowing when a question had been genuinely answered and when it had only been deferred. She knew the difference. She had apparently decided this was the first kind.

"Okay," she said. She picked up Gerald and her school bag. "I'm going to start drafting the review."

Daniel listened to her footsteps down the hall, the particular sound of a six-year-old with something to do. He kept cooking. The pan was the right temperature now. The light was coming in through the kitchen window at the angle that meant it was around five o'clock, the end of the afternoon sliding toward evening.

He thought about what she had said. Not about Leonardo, not about the governance review, not even about Inês's cat, which he hoped was fine and probably was. He thought about the observation that had been delivered between the site inspection report and the question about prayer, delivered, he noticed, in the same register, as one of a series of things she had been carrying since morning and was now putting down.

She had noticed. She had been filing it, the way she filed things, not urgently, not with distress, with the patient methodical attention of a child who was building a picture and had learned that the picture took time.

He thought about that, and said nothing about it to anyone, and finished making dinner.
