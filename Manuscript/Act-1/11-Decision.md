# Chapter 11: Decision

*The farmhouse kitchen. The Alentejo. Afternoon.*

Sarah was at the counter when he came in, going through the day's mail with the particular efficiency she used for tasks she had already decided not to think about too hard. Bills in one pile. Everything else in another. She looked up before he said anything. She always did, when he crossed a threshold with something on him.

"What."

"Kowalski called." He pulled out a chair but did not sit in it yet, one hand resting on the back of it instead, and she recognized that too: a man arranging himself before he delivered something he had already arranged in his own head first. "It wasn't his usual day, and it wasn't anything like his usual register."

She set the mail down. The clock above the stove read 3:15.

"He's sending someone," Daniel said. "A woman. Her name is Branca."

"Sending her here."

"Yes."

Sarah waited. She had learned, across two years of watching him relay Kowalski's calls, that Daniel gave information in the order it mattered to him, not the order that would satisfy someone else's curiosity fastest, and that the fastest way through was to let him finish rather than steer him.

"He said she's been carrying something he can't reach. He's given her everything he knows how to give and none of it has landed. He doesn't know exactly what she needs. He knows he doesn't have it." Daniel finally sat, elbows on the table, and looked at his own hands for a second before he looked back up. "He asked if I understood what he was telling me. I said yes. I'm not sure I did."

"What aren't you sure about."

"The way he sounded." He turned that over before he found the shape of it. "Kowalski reasons things out loud, usually. Step by step, so you can follow the seams. This wasn't that. This was closer to a man telling you rain is coming because he can feel it in a knee, not because he's read a forecast." He paused. "He also said something to himself after, in Polish. I didn't ask him to translate it. That's not something he offers me and I don't take it."

Sarah pulled out the chair across from him and sat, which was its own small announcement: whatever this was, it had her whole attention now, not the mail-sorting fraction of it.

"That's what he said," Daniel said. "Here's what he didn't. He didn't say what she's carrying. He didn't say how he knows Figueiredo, or how much Figueiredo knows, or how much this Branca will know when she gets here. He told me we're safe, and he told her that too, and then he stopped. That particular stop, when a thing is not his to say and he'd rather leave silence in the gap than fill it with a guess."

"So we know a name."

"We know a name and a temperature." Daniel exhaled, something between a laugh and not one. "He said to be gentle with her. He said whatever this is has already cost her a great deal, and that she doesn't know yet she's come looking for anything."

Sarah was quiet for a moment, turning the sentence the same careful distance she turned data before she trusted it. "That's not nothing."

"No."

"It's also not a plan."

"No," he agreed. "It's Kowalski asking us to receive someone the same way we were received, before we understood what we were being received into."

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, through the kitchen window, the courtyard had gone the color it went every day around this hour, the stone warming from the inside out rather than the outside in, and neither of them moved to acknowledge it.

"We should talk about the cost," Sarah said, because that was the shape her caution took: naming the thing plainly rather than letting it sit unspoken and do its work in the dark. "We built a careful life here. Careful for a reason. Every person who comes through that gate is a person who could, eventually, put a shape to what's in the cellar, even if they never mean to."

"I know."

"Kowalski knows it better than we do. He's the one who taught us to be careful."

"I know that too."

"So we should be careful about this."

"We should," Daniel said. "And we're going to say yes anyway."

Sarah didn't argue the point, because he had said out loud the thing she had already arrived at somewhere beneath the part of her mind that built arguments, and there was no honest way to pretend she needed more time to get there. They both knew what the shape of a real deliberation felt like in this house. This did not have that shape. This had the shape of a decision that had already been made and was only now catching up to being spoken.

"Why does it feel easy," she said, "when it shouldn't."

"Because the alternative isn't actually available to either of us. Not really." Daniel turned his coffee cup a quarter turn without drinking from it, an old habit from a hundred interviews where the gesture bought him a second to find the true sentence instead of the fast one. "I keep thinking about what it cost me, doing this work for years and never being the one who got to receive anyone. I only ever got to ask questions and write down the answers. Now someone gets to come here and just be received, without having to earn it first with a good quote." He shook his head slightly, at himself. "That's not a reason. That's just what's true when I ask myself why the yes feels light instead of heavy."

Sarah didn't answer that right away. She was doing the thing she did instead of answering quickly, which was sit inside a feeling until she could name where in her body it had started.

Her yes had not arrived like his, built out of two years of practice at holding the ordinary open for whoever needed to walk through it. Hers had come from somewhere lower than that, somewhere closer to the register she used to trust before she had learned to distrust it, back before Harvard, back before she'd decided that a scientist's convictions had to earn their keep the same way an object's provenance did. She could not have said what evidence supported it. She only knew that when Daniel had said the name Branca, something in her had already answered before the rest of her had finished hearing the sentence.

Her hand had moved to her jacket pocket without her deciding to send it there. She only noticed once it had already arrived: the familiar weight against her fingers, the smooth cool grain of it, two years of carrying it from pocket to pocket until her hand no longer needed her permission to go looking for it. She held still for a second, feeling it the way she'd check a pulse, and then drew her hand back out, empty, and set it flat on the table where she could see it.

"Sarah."

"I'm here." She looked up at him. "If Kowalski says she needs to come, she comes."

She said it the way she said the things she had already tested and found solid, without the qualifying clauses she usually built around anything she cared about, and Daniel heard the difference: not a conclusion she had reasoned her way to, but one she had simply arrived at, the same way water finds the low ground without being told which way is down.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay," she said back, and reached for the mail again, because there was nothing left to decide and the bills still needed sorting, and because that was also, in its way, an answer: the ordinary resuming itself around a thing that had just been settled.
