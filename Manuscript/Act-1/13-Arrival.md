# Chapter 13: Arrival

*The farmhouse. The Alentejo. Dusk.*

Sarah heard the gravel before she saw the car, the particular unhurried crunch of a vehicle taking the lane slowly out of courtesy rather than necessity. She was already in the courtyard, had been for the last twenty minutes on the excuse of checking the tomato stakes, which did not need checking. The light had gone the gold and amber it went every evening at this hour, the farmhouse walls holding onto the day's warmth a little longer than the air around them, and she stood in it and waited without letting herself look like she was waiting.

Figueiredo's car stopped at the gate. He got out first, unhurried as always, and lifted a hand to her in the small economical wave that was as much greeting as he ever gave. Then the driver's door opened on the other side, and a woman got out.

Sarah had built no picture of her beforehand. She had refused to, on principle, the same discipline she used with an unopened inquiry: better to meet the object than the idea of it. Even so, something in her had expected someone smaller than the woman who straightened up on the far side of the car, a canvas bag over one shoulder, dark hair pulled back plainly, dressed for travel rather than for arriving anywhere in particular.

Branca held a piece of paper in one hand, soft at the folds, the particular softness of something carried a long time without being read again because the person carrying it already knows what it says. Kowalski's handwriting, Sarah guessed, though she couldn't have said how she knew that from this distance. Whatever it told her, it seemed to be the whole of what she had been given.

Figueiredo said something to her, low, and Branca nodded and came through the gate ahead of him, which was its own kind of information: Figueiredo did not walk in front of people he was delivering somewhere. He let them arrive on their own authority.

"Dr. Davidson." Branca's voice was quiet, careful, a voice trained not to take up more room than necessary. "I'm sorry. I don't know exactly what I'm supposed to say."

"You don't have to say anything." Sarah stepped forward and did the thing that felt right, which was not an embrace, not yet, but a hand extended, warm and ordinary. "Sarah is fine. Welcome."

Branca's hand, when it met hers, was steady. Her grip was practiced, the kind of handshake developed by someone who had spent years greeting frightened families at the worst hour of their lives and had learned exactly how much pressure reassured without overwhelming. Sarah noticed this the way she noticed things in an object under her hands: composition first, meaning later.

Figueiredo set a second, smaller bag inside the gate. "I have another stop before dark," he said, to both of them and neither in particular. He looked at Sarah for a moment longer than the sentence required. "She is in good hands here."

"She'll be well looked after."

He nodded, once, the same small economy he'd given from the car. Then he was through the gate and back behind the wheel, and the engine sound faded down the lane, leaving the courtyard to its evening quiet.

"Is there somewhere I should put this?" Branca asked, lifting the bag slightly.

"I'll show you the room." Sarah picked up the second bag before Branca could reach for it. "It's simple. I hope that's all right."

"It's more than all right." Branca said it plainly, without the reflexive gratitude of someone performing politeness. She meant it as a fact, not a courtesy.

They went in through the kitchen door. Sarah led her down the hall to the small room at the back, the one with the window that looked out over the courtyard wall to the field beyond, the olive trees just visible as darker shapes now that the light was going. She had aired it that morning and put clean linens on the bed, small preparations that had felt, at the time, like the least she could do for someone about whom she knew almost nothing.

"Is there anything you need? Water, or something to eat, whenever you're ready?"

"Not yet. Thank you." Branca set her bag on the end of the bed, not opening it, just setting it there the way you set something down when you are not sure yet how long you'll be staying. She looked at the window, the darkening field beyond it, and something in her shoulders loosened by a degree so small that Sarah might have missed it if she had not spent two years learning to read small changes in objects that gave up their information slowly.

"Is there anything I can help with? While I'm here." Branca turned back to her. "I don't want to be a guest who only takes up space."

"You won't be. But there's nothing tonight. Rest, if you can."

Branca nodded, and something in the nod suggested she had heard this kind of instruction before and had never quite managed to follow it, and did not expect to manage it tonight either.

Sarah left her to unpack what little there was and went back down the hall, and it was only once she was alone in the corridor that she let herself exhale the breath she had apparently been holding since the gravel first sounded on the lane.

* * *

Daniel had watched the last part of it from the writing-room doorway, close enough to see, far enough not to be seen watching. Jessie was already asleep, her door pulled to but not latched, and the house had the particular held quiet of evening after a child has gone down and before the adults have decided what to do with the rest of the night.

He had not meant to study the woman so closely. It happened anyway, the old habit from years of interviews, of watching how a person's body told a truer story than whatever they were prepared to say out loud. Branca did not move like someone in crisis. She moved like someone who had trained her body, over a long stretch of ordinary days, to occupy exactly the amount of space a room could spare her. Every gesture arrived a fraction smaller than it needed to be: the handshake offered but not extended too far, the bag set down rather than dropped, the nod that closed a sentence before it had fully finished being polite.

He had seen grief before, in the years of the beat he used to cover, the sudden kind and the slow kind both, and this was neither. Grief announced itself, even when a person tried to keep it quiet: a catch in the breath, a held stillness at the wrong moment, and eventually, given enough time, some kind of leak. What he was watching instead looked less like a wound and more like a skeleton. Whatever this woman carried had stopped being an event a long time ago and had become, instead, the shape she moved in. It was load-bearing now. She had built the rest of herself around it and learned to walk without favoring the old break.

He looked past Branca's doorway to where Sarah stood in the hall, watching the same thing he was watching, though he doubted she knew he was there to see her do it. Sarah's face had the particular stillness she wore when she was reading an object and had not yet decided what she thought it meant: attentive, unhurried, withholding judgment until the evidence was in. She was not looking at a grieving woman. He could see that much in the set of her mouth. She was looking at something else, something she had not named yet even to herself, and filing it the careful way she filed everything, for later, when there would be time to know what she had seen.

He did not go out into the hall. There was nothing yet that needed him there, and he had learned, across two years in this house, that some evenings asked to be witnessed rather than joined. He stood a moment longer in the doorway, then went back to the desk, where the notebook sat open and blank, and did not write anything down. Some things did not become sentences yet. They only became the reason you sat still and paid attention.
