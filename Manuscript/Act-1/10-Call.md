# Chapter 10: Call

*The farmhouse. The Alentejo. Afternoon.*

The phone rang at the wrong time.

Daniel knew this before he looked at it, the way you know a knock at the door is not the person you were expecting. Kowalski called every two or three weeks, always in the same window, late morning in Rome, which put it mid-morning here. It was well past that now. The light through the writing-room window had the flat, settled quality of early afternoon, the hour when the courtyard stones stopped taking on new heat and started simply holding what they had.

He picked it up anyway.

"Daniel."

"It's not Thursday," Daniel said. Not an accusation. Kowalski would hear it correctly.

"No." A pause, and in it Daniel could picture the whole small ritual: the glasses coming off, the cloth, the particular stillness of a man deciding how much of a thing to say and how much to let arrive on its own. "I am sending someone."

Daniel did not ask who, not yet. There was a way Kowalski said things that told you whether a question would be answered before he was ready, and this was not that kind of pause.

"Her name is Branca," Kowalski said. "She has been carrying something I cannot reach."

"Reach how?"

"I have given her the sacraments. I have given her the counsel I know how to give. I have given her time, which is usually the thing that works when nothing else does." Something in his voice was not analysis. Daniel had heard Kowalski reason through a hundred small crises over two years, methodical, Jesuit, each proposition tested before it left his mouth. This was not that register. This was closer to the way a man describes weather he can feel coming before any instrument has confirmed it. "None of it has reached her. I don't fully know what she needs. I only know I don't have it."

"Okay," Daniel said.

"Do you understand what I am telling you?"

He did, and he didn't, both at once, which was probably the honest answer to give a priest who had learned a long time ago not to trust certainty that arrived too quickly. "Yes," he said anyway, because it was also true in the way that mattered.

A silence opened on the line, not empty. Daniel had learned, over two years of these calls, that Kowalski's silences did work the way another man's sentences did. He waited it out.

"She will contact Figueiredo," Kowalski said. "I have told her you are safe. I did not tell her why, or what that means, or what she will find. I told her the truth I am permitted to tell her, which is only that she will not be asked to be anything she is not."

"When?"

"Soon. I don't know exactly. She is not moving quickly toward this. She is moving the way people move toward things they are afraid will disappoint them." Another pause, shorter this time, and then, quieter: "Be gentle with her, Daniel. Whatever this is, it has cost her a great deal already, and she does not know yet that she has come looking for anything."

He said something after that in Polish, low, not meant for translation, and Daniel let it pass the way he always did, the way you let a man finish a private sentence in his own house.

"I'll tell Sarah," Daniel said.

"Yes. Tell Sarah." A beat. "And Daniel. Thank you for not asking me more than I have."

The line went dead before Daniel could decide whether that required an answer.

He sat holding the phone a while after, the screen gone dark in his hand, and did not immediately get up. The notebook was still open on the desk where he'd left it that morning, the page blank below a single crossed-out line he hadn't managed to improve on. He did not go back to it. He turned instead toward the window, the way he did most days without deciding to, and looked at the courtyard wall.

Leonardo's stone was empty. He noted this the way he noted most things now, filed for later delivery to Jessie, who would want the report and would have theories about what it meant. He did not have theories of his own. He had a name he had never heard before this afternoon and a set of instructions so spare they amounted to almost nothing: someone was coming, she did not know what she needed, and he was not to ask her more than she offered.

It was not so different, he thought, from most of what the last two years had asked of him.

The light moved a little on the stone while he sat there, the particular slow drift of early afternoon into late, and he let it move without getting up to meet it. He was aware of not writing, which was its own kind of attention. He thought about the register Kowalski's voice had carried, that quality of weather sensed rather than reasoned, and about how rarely, in two years, he had heard that man sound like someone who did not already know the shape of the thing he was describing.

He thought: *she does not know yet that she has come looking for anything.*

He did not know what to do with the sentence except carry it, the way he had learned to carry things in this house, until it was time to set it down in front of someone else.

After a while, he could not have said exactly how long, only that the light had gone from flat to slanting and the courtyard had taken on the particular gold that meant the afternoon was ending whether he attended to it or not, he got up. He left the notebook closed on the desk. He needed to find Sarah.
