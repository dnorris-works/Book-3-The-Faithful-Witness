# Chapter 5: The Arrangement

*The farmhouse. The Alentejo. Morning.*

The message had come through the channel the day before — not a request, just a note in the standard logistics format: *will be passing Thursday, can stop if convenient.* Sarah had replied that Thursday was convenient. There was nothing more to arrange.

She was in the kitchen when she heard the car on the lane, and she went out to the courtyard gate rather than waiting for a knock. Figueiredo was the kind of man who appreciated being met rather than making someone come to a door.

He was older than she had expected when she first met him, though she could not have said what she had expected. He drove a dark blue Peugeot that was several years old and very clean, and he dressed in the practical way of someone whose work took him between offices and fields and sacristies without any of those places being quite his natural habitat. He was carrying a padded envelope and a small paper bag that turned out to contain pastéis de nata from the bakery in the village, which he passed to her without ceremony as he came through the gate.

"The turnaround on the Évora inquiry," he said. "Still three days?"

"Still three days. The response went out yesterday."

He nodded. He was not a man who confirmed things he already knew — he had asked because it was the kind of question that left room for a no, and he wanted her to know that room existed.

They stood in the courtyard for a moment. The morning was clear, the kind of October clarity that would not last into winter, the light still warm enough to make the stone walls of the farmhouse look like something from a different century. Figueiredo glanced at the courtyard without appearing to glance at it — she had noticed he did this, a kind of ambient assessment that was not surveillance exactly, more the habit of someone who had spent a long time being responsible for things.

He held out the padded envelope. "From a collector in Porto. He came through Padre Mendes, who came through the Braga chapter. The provenance documentation is inside but he believes it is incomplete."

Sarah took the envelope. It was sealed with tape over the flap and had the weight of something small and dense inside — not a photograph, then. An object, or a cast of one.

"He wants authentication of the object itself, not the documentation?"

"He wants to know what the object is." Figueiredo said this in the particular register he used when a requester's question was more interesting than they knew. "He has documentation that says it is a reliquary fragment, fourth or fifth century, North African. He believes this is correct. He wants to know what else is true about it."

Sarah turned the envelope in her hands. "I'll take a look at the documentation today."

"No urgency. He has been carrying it for some time. A few more days will not change anything." He paused. "He is not young. If there is something to be found, sooner would be better than later."

She understood this. There were requests that came from people for whom the answer had a particular kind of weight — not scholars, not institutions, but individuals for whom the inquiry was connected to something they needed to settle before they ran out of time to settle it. Figueiredo was good at recognizing these. He routed them with care.

"I'll prioritize it," she said.

He gave a small nod. "Is there anything you need?"

"No." She held up the pastéis. "These are welcome."

Something shifted in his face — not quite a smile, something more restrained than that, the expression of a man who does not often allow expressions. "My wife makes them better. But the village bakery is on the way."

He did not ask about the house. He did not ask about Daniel or Jessie, whose existence he presumably knew about, or about the equipment in the basement, which he certainly knew about in some general sense — a man did not route sensitive authentication requests to a farmhouse in the Alentejo without understanding that something was there to do the authenticating. He did not ask what it was. He did not ask how it worked.

This was the thing about Figueiredo that she had been trying to understand since the beginning of the arrangement: whether his restraint was trained or natural. Most people who dealt in sensitive information developed a professional incuriosity — they learned not to ask because asking was a security risk, because information you didn't have couldn't be extracted from you. That was one kind of restraint. But there was another kind, rarer, that came from something more like respect — from understanding that the thing being kept was not yours to know, and that understanding this was itself a form of faithfulness.

She had watched him for two years across their intermittent exchanges, and she had come to think it was the second kind. He was not incurious. He was someone who had decided, at some point long before he met her, that certain kinds of knowing were not his to pursue. The decision was not fresh. It had the quality of something practiced into a disposition.

"Thursday will work for future visits as well," she said. "If that's useful."

"Thursdays are generally manageable." He reached for the gate. "I will be in the area again in three weeks. I'll send a note."

She thanked him. He gave another of those small nods and went back through the gate to the car, and she listened to the engine on the lane until it faded.

She stood in the courtyard for a moment with the envelope in one hand and the pastéis in the other. The fig tree shifted in a light wind off the field. Leonardo was on the lower stone — the secondary position he had apparently adopted, now two days into what Jessie was formally treating as an established presence pending the outcome of the review. A territorial assessment in progress.

Sarah looked at the padded envelope. A collector in Porto, not young, carrying something he needed to understand before he ran out of time. Someone who had found his way through two intermediaries to a channel that could tell him what else was true about an object he had been living with.

She went inside and down to the cellar.

* * *

The documentation was what Figueiredo had suggested: present but incomplete. There was a typed provenance note on paper that had the particular yellowing of something printed in the nineties, a photograph of the object from two angles, and a handwritten note from what appeared to be a previous owner — not the collector, someone before him — that described the object as having been in the family for three generations and acquired, the writer believed, in Alexandria in the early twentieth century.

The object itself was wrapped in acid-free tissue inside a small rigid box: a fragment of carved bone, roughly the size of a matchbook, with incised decoration that under the photograph's light showed clearly — a chi-rho monogram flanked by two figures she could not make out at this resolution.

She set the documentation on the table and opened her laptop. She described what she saw in the photograph to MEMORIA, and MEMORIA was quiet for a moment.

Then it said: the chi-rho flanked by figures of this type was common in North African Christian contexts from the late third century onward. The specific proportions of the monogram visible in this photograph were consistent with Egyptian workshop production, likely Alexandrian, from the fourth or early fifth century. The provenance note was consistent with this.

"The collector wants to know what else is true about it," Sarah said.

A longer pause.

"The carving has a quality that is not visible in the photograph," MEMORIA said. "I cannot determine it from an image. The object would need to come here."

Sarah looked at the photograph. The fragment was small enough to travel easily. A padded envelope, a secure channel, three days each way.

"I'll ask Figueiredo to arrange it."

She made the note in the channel and closed the laptop. The three green lights burned. Through the ventilation gap she could hear the courtyard — wind in the fig tree, the ordinary sounds of a morning in the Alentejo going about its business. Somewhere in the village the church bell marked the half hour, faint and specific, the way sounds carried in that kind of air.

She sat in the cellar for a few minutes more, not thinking about anything particular, before going back upstairs to make coffee and open the pastéis.
