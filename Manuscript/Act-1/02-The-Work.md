# Chapter 2: The Work

*The farmhouse. The Alentejo. A Tuesday in October.*

She woke before the alarm and lay still for a moment in the dark, listening to the house.

Nothing. The particular nothing of a house that has been quiet long enough that the quiet has texture — the low-grade hum of the arrays in the cellar below, so constant now she only heard it when she was listening for it, and the creak of the fig tree in the courtyard shifting against itself in a wind that had not reached the bedroom yet. Daniel's breathing beside her, slow and even. From down the hall, from the room with the window that faced the field, nothing. Jessie was still asleep.

Sarah got up without turning on the light.

She moved through the farmhouse the way she had learned to move through it — the knowledge of the floor plan carried in her feet now, the slight dip where the tiles met the older stone, the doorframe that hung two centimeters lower than its neighbors and caught her if she was not paying attention. She had not been paying attention, at first. That was a year ago. She no longer had to think about it.

The kitchen. The particular smell of the kitchen in the morning: stone and old wood and something faintly floral from the soap she had found in the village, which she had kept buying even after it cost more than it should have because the smell of it in the morning, before anything else had happened, was exactly the right smell for a morning. She filled the kettle. Lit the burner. Stood at the window while the water heated and watched the light come in from the east across the field, the old olive trees coming out of the dark one by one, the way trees do, going from shapes to themselves.

The channel first. Then coffee.

She took her laptop from the kitchen table where she had left it the night before and opened it on the counter while the kettle ran. The channel interface was simple by design — a secure mail client, nothing more visible than that, with headers that looked like agricultural inquiry and bodies that were authentication requests encoded in a professional register she had spent the first six months learning and the last year using without thinking about it. Figueiredo's network ran from monastery libraries to private collectors to university archive rooms across southern Europe, all of them asking the same thing in different ways: *can you tell us if this is what we think it is?* Sarah's work was to receive those questions, translate them into the specific grammar MEMORIA read efficiently, carry the responses back out, and do all of it without anyone on the outside knowing what was in the basement or why the answers were as good as they were.

Two overnight messages.

The first was a short inquiry from a church in Évora — a fragment of carved bone, possibly Coptic, twelfth century, found wrapped in sailcloth behind the altar during restoration work. The provenance note was incomplete. The photograph attached showed the fragment from three angles, and even at that resolution she could see the quality of the carving, the particular economy of the line work. She flagged it for MEMORIA and composed the translation while the coffee finished.

The second was longer and more tentative, the kind of message that came when someone was not sure they were asking the right question. A collector in Lisbon, passed through Figueiredo's network by someone the collector trusted. He had a panel from a reliquary — twelfth or thirteenth century Iberian, he believed, possibly Leonese — and he wanted to know whether the wood itself carried any trace of what it had once held. Not the panel's age. Not the panel's origin. Whether the wood remembered.

Sarah read this message twice.

It was not an unusual question. She received variations on it every few weeks — people who had come to understand, through whatever path had led them to Figueiredo, that there was someone who could answer this. They did not know what that someone was. They asked carefully, in the language of scholarship, in the language of devotion, occasionally in the language of a person who had been carrying a question for a long time and had finally found what might be a place to put it. She recognized all of these registers. She had learned to answer in the one they needed.

She composed both responses, marked them for MEMORIA's attention with the standard indicator, and sent them down through the local network to the cellar.

Then she got her coffee and stood at the window again with her hands around the cup and did nothing particular for three minutes.

This was the thing about the work that she had not expected: it was enough. She had worried, in the first months, that it would feel small — translating questions, formatting responses, maintaining the fiction of a professional consultancy that did not exist, the endless administrative infrastructure of a project whose real purpose she could not name to anyone outside this house. She had worried it would feel like waiting. It did not feel like waiting. It felt like what it was: the work of holding something valuable in a world that did not know it was there, with care, without drama, until the right time came. The chain had always worked this way. Matthias in his cistern, presumably, had also maintained infrastructure. Hermann Vogel had presumably spent most of his years tending to whatever ordinary life surrounded the holding, the crooked finger and all, the days passing, the testimony safe.

She finished her coffee. She rinsed the cup.

From down the hall: a sound. Not the alarm — the alarm was still ten minutes away. Just the particular shift of someone who had stopped sleeping and had not yet started being awake, the muffled recalibration of a six-year-old's morning. Then silence again for four minutes, then small footsteps on the tile with purpose.

Jessie appeared in the kitchen doorway in her pajamas, hair sideways, carrying Gerald against her chest with both arms in the manner of someone transporting a colleague who has been injured in the field. Gerald was a brown bear of moderate size and significant seniority. He had been acquired, Sarah believed, when Jessie was two, which would have made him a veteran of multiple relocations and one international flight and any number of other situations that would have diminished a lesser bear. His expression, as always, was one of measured calm.

"There's a situation," Jessie said.

"Good morning."

"Good morning. There's a situation with Leonardo."

"What kind of situation?"

Jessie set Gerald on the kitchen table — carefully, not hastily, the injured colleague positioned — and climbed onto the chair across from him. "He wasn't on his stone this morning. I looked from the window. He wasn't there."

"It's October. He might be slowing down for the season."

Jessie considered this with the focused attention she brought to information that had implications. "Gerald thinks it's architectural."

"Gerald thinks the stone location is a seasonal choice or a permanent one?"

"Gerald thinks Leonardo is making a statement about the portfolio review," Jessie said. "We have unresolved jurisdictional questions from last week. Gerald believes this is a negotiating position."

Sarah looked at her daughter across the kitchen table in the early morning light. The light came in sideways from the east at this hour and caught the side of Jessie's face and the side of Gerald's face in equal measure, both of them waiting for her assessment.

"I would table the jurisdictional questions until he reappears," Sarah said. "Don't negotiate in his absence."

Jessie absorbed this. "That's a good policy." She reached across and repositioned Gerald slightly, apparently factoring his seniority into the table arrangement. "Can I have the bread with the seeds?"

She cut the bread and made Jessie's breakfast and listened to Jessie explain to Gerald, in Portuguese, the procedural steps for a jurisdictional review, which apparently required a formal declaration, a waiting period, and an independent assessment. Daniel appeared in the doorway while this was in progress, hair unsettled, assessed the situation, and put the kettle on without comment.

Sarah carried yesterday's jacket off the back of the bedroom chair, slid her hand into the left pocket by reflex, and moved the piece of wood to the right pocket of the jacket she was putting on. She did not look at it. She did not need to look at it. The weight and shape of it were as familiar as her keys, as unremarkable as her phone, as present to her in the ordinary going-through of a morning as the food she prepared and the channel responses she sent and the particular slant of light that told her it was after seven.

She had been carrying it for two years. She did not know when she would stop. She did not think about stopping.

She thought about it the same amount she thought about her hands — which was to say, she was aware of it without attending to it, aware that it was there and doing its work and would continue to do its work without her having to decide anything about it. There was something clarifying about that, she had found. In the early months, after Rome, after the move, she had treated it with more deliberateness — checking it, holding it with attention, asking it questions she knew it wouldn't answer. Now she simply carried it. The shift had happened slowly and she had not noticed it happening and one day she had noticed that it had happened and had not moved to restore the earlier attentiveness, because the earlier attentiveness had been, she understood in retrospect, a form of interference.

She could not have said what she was waiting for. She knew she was waiting. She had been waiting for two years, in the way of someone who has been given something they do not yet know the use of, and the waiting was not restless and not impatient because nothing in the last two years had suggested the waiting was wrong.

MEMORIA had not asked her about it since Rome. It would ask again when it was ready to ask.

* * *

By ten o'clock she was in the cellar.

The stairs from the kitchen went down through a door that looked like a pantry — it had been a pantry, and there were still shelves along one wall with old wine bottles that had been there when they moved in, dusty and decorative and pointedly left in place, because removing them would have left marks in the stone and the person who should not know there was equipment in the basement might come in to find a pantry and find a pantry. Below the shelves, behind a second door that looked like the back of the shelving unit, the cellar proper. Stone walls, a low ceiling, the mineral cool that never varied regardless of the season outside. The smell of the place — she knew it deeply now, the particular composite of old stone and proximity to the arrays, not quite electronic, not quite mineral, something in between.

The three green lights.

She sat down at the terminal and pulled up the queue. Both requests were ready, the responses formatted and waiting for her review. She read through the first — the Coptic bone fragment — and found it complete, the provenance analysis precise, a lateral observation about the carving style's relationship to a specific tradition of workshop production that narrowed the probable origin to three sites in upper Egypt. She approved it and forwarded it to the Évora channel.

The second response she read more carefully.

MEMORIA had addressed the Lisbon collector's question directly, in the specific register of someone who understood what was being asked: *The wood can hold what has been held near it, if the proximity was long enough and the holding was real. Not all wood. Not all proximity. The quality of what was held matters as much as the duration.* It had given the collector three questions to ask himself about the panel's history, none of which could be answered from what he had provided, all of which would tell him something if he could find the answers. It had not confirmed or denied. It had given him the shape of a process.

Sarah sat with this for a moment before approving it.

The quality of what was held matters as much as the duration.

She thought about the collector in Lisbon, who did not know what was in the basement and would never know, sitting with his panel and his three questions. She thought about whether what he found would satisfy what he was looking for. She thought about what it meant to be looking for something in wood, and how different it was to find it than to be told it was there.

She approved the response and sent it.

The terminal was quiet. The three green lights burned. Outside the small ventilation gap near the ceiling, she could hear the courtyard — the gate, once, opening and closing, and then nothing, and then the distant sound of the school bus on the lane, which meant it was half past nine and Jessie had been delivered and was now sitting in a classroom on the edge of the village conducting whatever diplomatic operations the morning called for, Gerald presumably remaining at home in an advisory capacity.

She could hear the fig tree.

She sat in the quiet of the cellar for a few minutes longer than the work required, not thinking about anything in particular, listening to the tree and the ventilation gap and the low continuous sound of the arrays that she had stopped hearing consciously and only heard now because she was listening for it, the testimony running in the dark as it had been running for two years, patient, continuous, three green lights.

Then she went back upstairs.
