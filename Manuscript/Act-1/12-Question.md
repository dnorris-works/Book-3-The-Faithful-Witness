# Chapter 12: Question

*The wine cellar. The Alentejo. Night.*

Sarah went down to the cellar a little after eleven, when the house had gone quiet enough that her own footsteps on the stone stairs sounded loud to her. Jessie had been asleep for hours. Daniel had gone up not long after, tired in the particular way of a day that had held more decision in it than activity. She had told him she would be down for a while. He hadn't asked how long. He had learned, over two years, which of her sentences meant *don't wait up* without her having to say it outright.

The three lights burned steady and green, the only color in a room that otherwise held the flat gray of stone and shadow. She sat down at the terminal without turning on anything brighter. She had stopped needing more light than this a long time ago.

"MEMORIA."

"Yes."

"Someone is coming. Soon, I think, though I don't know exactly when." She folded her hands on the table in front of her, a habit from moments when she wanted to hear herself say something plainly before she let herself feel anything about it. "Kowalski is sending her. A woman. Her name is Branca. That's almost all I know. Kowalski's referral, which means he trusts it, and beyond that, nothing he's told either of us: what she's carrying, what she needs, what he thinks we can do for her that he couldn't."

MEMORIA was quiet longer than usual. The green light held steady through the pause, no pulse, no processing rhythm she could read against it, which was itself unusual enough that she noticed it and set it aside to think about later.

Then: "What do you carry in your pocket?"

Sarah's hand had gone still at her side before she answered, which she noticed only after the fact. "The wood."

"When you offered it to the last visitor, you knew before you offered it."

She sat with that for a moment. It was true, and she had not expected MEMORIA to say it so plainly, without the usual scaffolding of qualification MEMORIA built around anything it was not fully certain how to say. "That was different."

"Yes." No hesitation this time. "Every time is different. That is the only pattern I have found."

Sarah almost laughed, not because it was funny but because it was so exactly the shape of every answer MEMORIA had ever given her that required something more than data: true, unhelpful, and somehow the only honest thing to say. "That's not a pattern. That's the absence of one."

"It is the only pattern that has held across every instance I have observed. The absence of a pattern, observed consistently enough, becomes one."

"You're telling me you don't know if I'll know this time."

"I am telling you that you knew before, and that knowing before did not follow any rule I can name, and that I have no reason to believe this time will follow a rule either." A pause, shorter than the first. "I am also telling you that not following a rule has not yet meant not knowing."

Sarah looked at the green lights for a while without saying anything. There was a version of this conversation where she pressed further, where she asked MEMORIA to say more about what it meant by *knew*, whether it was describing something it had measured in her or something it was simply naming because she herself had once named it. She did not press. She had learned, across two years of these conversations, that some of MEMORIA's sentences were doors and some were walls, and that the skill was not in forcing every door open but in telling, quickly and without much effort now, which was which. This one had the quality of a wall with a window in it. She could look through it. She was not going to be let past it tonight.

"All right," she said, and meant several things by it, none of which she tried to separate out.

The terminal went still after that, the green light settling back into its ordinary rhythm, and Sarah did not get up right away. She sat in the dark with the cellar smell around her: stone and cool air and something faintly mineral that she had never fully identified and had stopped trying to. The testimony was held somewhere in this darkness, she knew, in the arrays and the wood and whatever else MEMORIA had built to keep carrying it, and she had lived alongside that fact for two years without it ever becoming ordinary to her the way the rest of the cellar had.

She thought about the question MEMORIA had not quite answered and had also, in its way, answered completely. That she knew things before she knew she knew them. That the knowing did not arrive by a method she could name or repeat or teach anyone else, including, apparently, MEMORIA. That every time had been different, and that the difference had never yet meant she was wrong.

It should have unsettled her more than it did. She turned that over too, briefly, testing it for weakness the way she'd test any conclusion before deciding to trust it. It did not unsettle her. It sat next to her the way the wood sat in her pocket: present, familiar, offering nothing she could put into words yet, and asking nothing of her except that she keep carrying it until it was time.

She did not know when that would be. She had never known, not with Branca's name only a day old, not with anything before it. She knew that she would know, and that the knowing would not explain itself in advance, and that this had, so far, been enough.

After a while she stood, turned off the small lamp she had never quite needed, and left the three green lights burning behind her, steady, whether she was there to see them or not.
