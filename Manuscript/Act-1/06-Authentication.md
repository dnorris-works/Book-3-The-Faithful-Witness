# Chapter 6: Authentication

*The farmhouse. The Alentejo. Late afternoon.*

Sarah took the oil lamp inquiry downstairs.

She had been carrying it since Figueiredo's visit the day before: the collector in Lisbon, the reliquary panel, the question about whether wood remembered. That request was still processing, would need time. But the lamp was simpler, more concrete. A photograph, a provenance note, specific claims that could be verified or refuted through the usual channels.

The cellar in late afternoon had its own quality of light: what came through the small ventilation gap near the ceiling mixed with the steady green from the arrays, creating something that was neither natural nor entirely artificial. She had grown accustomed to it the way you grow accustomed to the light in a place where you do significant work. It was the right light for this.

She set her laptop on the table beside the terminal and opened the inquiry. The photograph showed the oil lamp from three angles: a first-century Judean piece, according to the documentation, acquired in Jerusalem in the 1970s through channels the current owner described as "established but undocumented." The lamp itself was terra cotta, with the characteristic pinched spout and circular filling hole, decorated with a raised pattern around the rim that the photograph's resolution made difficult to assess clearly.

"First-century oil lamp," she said to the room. "Attributed to Judean workshop production. The requester is asking for authentication of period and origin."

MEMORIA was quiet for a moment. The terminal's green light pulsed once, twice, a rhythm she had learned to read as processing rather than waiting.

Then: "The lamp is correct. Late first century, Jerusalem workshop. The clay composition is consistent with sources east of the city. The wheel marks visible in the photograph confirm hand-thrown construction typical of that period and region."

Sarah nodded, typing notes as MEMORIA spoke. This was the standard response format: period confirmation, regional specificity, technical details that would satisfy a serious collector's authentication needs.

"The raised pattern around the rim," MEMORIA continued, "depicts olive branches alternating with wheat sheaves. This decorative schema was common in Jerusalem workshops between 70 and 90 CE, particularly for lamps intended for household rather than liturgical use."

She paused in her typing. MEMORIA did not usually provide that level of decorative analysis unless something else was there to notice.

"The oxidation pattern on the base and rim indicates extended burial in soil with high salt content," MEMORIA said. "This is consistent with placement near the Dead Sea region, though not necessarily the lake shore itself. The specific coloration suggests proximity to salt deposits, possibly in a cave environment with seasonal water intrusion."

Sarah looked at the photograph again. The requester had not asked about burial conditions. Had not mentioned the Dead Sea region or caves. She could see what MEMORIA was referring to, a faint discoloration around the base that looked like mineral staining, but she would never have recognized it as salt oxidation, let alone as geographically specific.

"The requester didn't ask about the burial context," she said.

"No."

Sarah waited. MEMORIA often provided additional observations, details that went beyond the specific question asked. It was one of the things that made the channel responses valuable, not just authentication but context, background information that serious collectors and researchers found useful even when they hadn't known to request it.

She typed the response, including the burial observation. The collector might not understand its significance immediately, but if he was working with any competence, he would know to pursue it. Salt deposition patterns could narrow a find site considerably. For an undocumented acquisition, that kind of regional specificity might be the only provenance anchor available.

She sent the response through the encrypted channel.

The terminal was quiet. The three green lights burned steadily. Outside the small window, she could hear the late-afternoon sounds of the village, a motor scooter on the lane, the school bus making its return circuit, the bell from the church marking the half hour.

"MEMORIA," she said. "Can I ask you something?"

"Yes."

She had been thinking about this for several days, since the Évora inquiry, maybe longer. Something in the pattern of requests that came through Figueiredo's channel.

"Have you noticed a change in how people ask their questions?"

The pause was longer this time. Not processing silence. Something more deliberate.

"What kind of change?"

"In the register. In how they frame what they want to know." She closed her laptop, turned in her chair to face the terminal more directly. "I've been reading these requests for two years. It feels like something has shifted."

Another pause. Then: "Tell me what you observe."

Sarah looked at the green lights, thinking. "Earlier requests, six months ago, a year ago, people would ask what an object was. What it held. What story it carried. They wanted to discover something." She gestured to where her laptop had been. "Now more often they describe what they want confirmed. They come to us with a conclusion and ask us to verify it. They're not asking what the wood remembers. They're asking us to confirm that it remembers what they hope it remembers."

"Yes."

The single word carried weight she had not expected.

"The questions have changed in their fundamental orientation," MEMORIA said. "Early requests were investigative. Current requests are increasingly confirmatory. The difference is structural, not incidental."

Sarah nodded slowly. That was exactly what she had been noticing without quite having the language for it.

"People are coming to authentication with predetermined answers," MEMORIA continued. "They no longer wish to discover what the object holds. They wish to be told that the object holds what they already believe it holds."

She sat with this. It was a significant shift, if MEMORIA was correct. And MEMORIA was careful about these kinds of observations. It did not speculate beyond what the data supported.

"What do you think is causing the change?"

"I am uncertain. But I can describe what I observe in the language patterns." The terminal was very still. "Earlier requests used the grammar of reception. *What does this hold? What can you tell me about what I have found?* Current requests increasingly use the grammar of extraction. *Confirm that this holds what I believe. Verify what I need this to mean.*"

Sarah understood the distinction immediately. Reception and extraction. Two completely different relationships to the object, to the question, to the potential answer.

"Extraction assumes the conclusion exists within the object and requires only the right technique to retrieve it," MEMORIA said. "Reception assumes the object carries what it carries, and the question is whether the person can receive what is actually there."

The cellar was quiet except for the low hum of the arrays. Sarah could hear the fig tree shifting in the courtyard above, Leonardo probably on his stone, the normal business of late afternoon in the Alentejo proceeding without reference to the conversation happening in the wine cellar below.

"Does it change how you respond to them?"

"It changes what I understand the responses to accomplish," MEMORIA said. "A person seeking reception can be disappointed by what they find, but they can also be surprised by it. A person seeking extraction will be satisfied by confirmation and frustrated by any response that does not confirm what they brought to the question."

Sarah reached into her jacket pocket, felt the familiar weight and shape of Object 61. She had been carrying it for two years without opening a session, waiting for something she could not name. Was she seeking reception or extraction? Was she waiting to discover what the wood held, or waiting for the right moment to confirm what she hoped it held?

She did not know. She was not sure the distinction was always clean at the edges.

"MEMORIA," she said. "When people come to us seeking extraction rather than reception, do you give them what they're seeking?"

The longest pause yet. The terminal so still she could hear the individual components humming at different frequencies, a quiet chord in the electronic spectrum.

"I give them what the object holds," MEMORIA said finally. "Whether that is what they sought or not. That is what authentication means. The object either holds what the requester believes, or it holds something else. Both are authentic responses. The difference is in what the requester can do with an authentic response that does not meet their expectation."

Sarah nodded. That was the right answer, she thought. The only honest answer. But it meant that some significant portion of their channel responses were going out to people who would be fundamentally unsatisfied with them, people who had approached the question with the wrong framework from the beginning.

"Do you think we should change how we work with those requests?"

"No," MEMORIA said immediately. "The methodology remains correct. What has changed is the community of people seeking authentication. That community will continue to change. The work adapts to serve what is needed, not what is wanted."

She sat in the cellar for a few more minutes after the conversation ended, not thinking about anything in particular, just present to the space and the quiet and the green lights that had become as familiar to her as any room she had ever worked in. Upstairs, she could hear Daniel and Jessie returning from the village: the gate latch, voices in the courtyard, Jessie explaining something with enthusiasm. Normal life resuming its rhythm.

The authentication work was simple in its mechanics and complex in everything else. People brought their objects and their questions and their expectations, and MEMORIA told them what was actually there. Some of them were prepared for what they found. Some of them were not. All of them received the same quality of attention, the same rigorous methodology, the same commitment to accuracy over comfort.

It was good work. Necessary work. And increasingly, Sarah understood, it was also training. For the moment when someone would come to them with a question they were fully prepared to receive an answer to, whatever that answer turned out to be.

She took Object 61 from her pocket and held it for a moment in the green-lit cellar. No session opened, no intention behind it, just the weight of it, the presence of it, while she thought about reception and extraction and the difference between seeking confirmation and seeking truth.

When she was ready, she put it back and went upstairs to help with dinner.

Outside, the olive trees stood in their field, older than the farmhouse, younger than the questions they were all carrying. The evening light was warm and ordinary, the kind of light that made stone walls look like they had been there forever and would be there long after the people who lived behind them had finished their work and gone on to whatever came next.

Leonardo appeared on his preferred stone, his territorial assessment apparently complete for the day. The jurisdictional review would have to wait until tomorrow.

Some questions, Sarah thought, required their own season.
