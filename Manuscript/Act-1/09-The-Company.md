# Chapter 9: The Company

*Jessie's room. The Alentejo. Evening.*

The window in Jessie's room faced the field, and at eight o'clock the field still held enough light to see the shapes of the olive trees against it, not their color anymore, just their outlines, dark and irregular, the way trees look right before a house decides it is night. Sarah had left the shutters half open, the way Jessie liked them, and the evening air came in smelling of dry grass and the last of the day's heat coming up off the stone.

Jessie was in bed with the sheet pulled to her chest and Gerald propped against the pillow beside her, positioned, as far as Sarah could tell, to also see out the window. She had been quiet for longer than her usual bedtime quiet, which was never very long. Sarah had noticed this from the doorway and had come to sit on the edge of the bed rather than simply saying goodnight, because a silence that long, from Jessie, was usually the sound of a question being finished before it was spoken.

"Are those olive trees as old as the tree in the stories?" Jessie said, still looking at the window rather than at her mother. "The one that remembers things?"

Sarah did not answer right away. It was not a hard question to hold. She had held stranger ones from this particular six-year-old, delivered with the same flat directness, usually about Leonardo's jurisdiction or the exact mechanism by which Gerald's opinions were formed, but there was something in the specific shape of this one that made her want to get it right rather than get it out quickly.

"Those trees are old," she said. "Very old. But not that old." She nodded toward the window, toward the dark shapes standing in their rows beyond the gate. "The tree in the stories is much older. Older than the city we lived in before. Older than the buildings we visited in Rome."

Jessie turned this over. Sarah could see her doing it: the small stillness that meant her daughter was placing something on a scale she was still building, the way she had placed Leonardo's second sighting against his first, the way she placed everything now, methodically, before she let it settle.

"That's a lot older," Jessie said.

"It is."

She had not planned to say any of it, and she noticed, only after the words were out, that she had not softened them the way she softened most things for Jessie, the way she'd once turned a story about a difficult reliquary provenance into a story about a box that kept a secret safe for people who needed it kept. This had come out plain, the way a fact comes out when the person saying it has stopped remembering that it needs defending. She had answered her daughter the way she might have answered Figueiredo, or MEMORIA, or no one at all, the way you answer when you are simply reporting what you know to be true.

"Does it get lonely?" Jessie asked. "Being that old?"

The question came without weight, the way Jessie's biggest questions often did, dropped into the evening between one breath and the next as though it belonged with the olive trees and the window and Gerald's opinion on cold weather. Sarah felt herself reaching for an answer before she had decided what the answer should be, and what came was not a story and not a deflection.

"I think it has had a lot of company," she said.

Jessie considered this with her eyes already drifting toward closed, the particular expression she got when a fact had been received and filed and did not require any further defense. She reached over and adjusted Gerald an inch, the way she did when a piece of business needed to be formally handed off before she could rest.

"Gerald should think about that," she said, to Gerald rather than to Sarah, and then, apparently satisfied that the instruction had been properly issued, she closed her eyes.

Sarah stayed for a few minutes, watching her daughter's breathing lengthen and slow into the rhythm that meant she had actually gone under and not simply decided to look like she had, which was a different thing and one Jessie had attempted more than once. The room settled. Outside, a bird made one call and did not repeat it. The olive trees kept their shapes against the last of the light without needing anything from the house.

It had cost her nothing, that answer. That was the thing Sarah found herself sitting with once the room was quiet enough to sit with anything. Normally she measured what she gave her daughter, how much of Rome to explain, how much of the move, how much of why the gate had a lock that mattered, clipping the truth down until it fit. This time she had not measured anything. She had simply said what she knew, the way MEMORIA said what an object held whether or not the person asking was ready to receive it. *Older than the city we lived in before.* No metaphor. No scaffolding borrowed from Daniel's bedtime version of the tree. It had come out of her the way something comes out of a person once it has stopped being an idea and started being a fact they live inside.

She was not sure the answer had been wrong. What she noticed instead, late, the way she noticed most things about herself, well behind whatever had actually happened, was that she had given her daughter something true about the tree, plainly, without deciding in advance how much of it was safe to say. She had spoken as someone who simply knew. She could not remember deciding to become someone who simply knew.

Gerald had been repositioned again in his sleep, or Jessie had done it in the last seconds before going under; he faced the window now, toward the field, toward the dark shapes that were, Sarah understood as she looked at them herself, only pretending tonight to be the whole of what old could mean.

She sat a while longer in the near-dark, the field outside doing what it always did at this hour, holding its shape without any help from her, and then she got up carefully, the way you get up from a chair you don't want to wake, and went out and pulled the door most of the way closed behind her.
