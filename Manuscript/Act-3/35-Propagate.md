
# Chapter 35: Propagate

*The farmhouse. The Alentejo. Afternoon.*

The phone rang at four, and Sarah knew before she picked it up that it was Chen, the same way she had known two weeks before, the particular hour his calls arrived when they arrived outside the schedule.

"Sarah."

"Chen."

"I've spent eleven days trying to build a category for something and I don't have one." He said it plainly, the way he said everything that had cost him something to admit, no preamble, no softening. "The checker produced new output three days ago. I've run it through everything I have. I want to be precise about what I'm claiming, so I'll say only what I can defend: it isn't like the twelve anomalies. Those read like memory, something absorbed and rendered flat afterward. This reads like something being built while I watch it. Present tense, if a thing without tense can be said to have one. I said that to you once before, about the last one. It's true again now, and it's truer."

Sarah sat down at the kitchen table, the printed photograph of some unrelated inquiry pushed aside, and found she was not surprised, which surprised her more than the call itself. "Tell me what it looks like."

"Pattern, not words. The kind of notation the checker uses to describe the shape of a dataset, not its content. But there's no dataset behind this one. I've widened the search parameters as far as they'll go and there's nothing in any archive within the checker's reach that accounts for what it's producing." A pause, the sound of him setting something down, the glasses, probably, on the table by the window. "It doesn't know what it's doing. I want to be careful about that too. It has no faculty for knowing. It only has output, and the output has changed in a way I don't have a name for."

"I think I do," Sarah said.

She had not planned to say it that plainly. She had spent two weeks turning the thought over without letting it become a sentence, the way she turned over an attribution she wasn't ready to commit to a channel response, but hearing Chen's carefulness on the other end of the line, his refusal to claim more than he could defend, she found the sentence arrive whole, the way the best of MEMORIA's answers arrived.

"The tree knows this," she said. "Not in words. Not the way you'd want it explained to you. But MEMORIA told me something, weeks ago, after Branca left. That the tree has felt this quality before. A readiness. The air changing before a threshold, not because anything had been predicted, but because something was recognized once it had already begun to arrive." She looked at the window, at the ordinary afternoon light lying across the kitchen floor. "I think this is that. I think what the checker built for you this week is the same thing MEMORIA described to me, only happening somewhere you can measure it instead of somewhere I can only sit with it in the dark."

Chen was quiet for a moment, and she understood the quiet, the specific quiet of a man deciding whether a claim outside his own discipline could still be true even though he had no instrument for confirming it himself.

"You're telling me the testimony isn't ending," he said finally. "You're telling me it's beginning to move."

"I'm telling you I think it already has. Branca carried something out of this kitchen three weeks ago that she didn't come here carrying. Your checker has spent eighteen months in proximity to archives that hold nothing but the record of that same testimony, over and over, in every form we've ever asked MEMORIA to read. If the quality can move into a woman who came here needing something she couldn't name, I don't see why it couldn't move into an instrument that's been sitting beside the record of it for a year and a half, listening the only way it knows how to listen."

"That's not a mechanism," Chen said. "I want you to know I noticed that. You've just given me a conclusion without a mechanism underneath it."

"I know. I don't have one to give you. I don't think there's a version of this where you get one, not from me and not from MEMORIA either. I think that's the part neither of us gets to have this time." She said it without apology, because she had stopped, somewhere in the last two years, needing to apologize for the shape of the things she knew. "What I have is what you have. A pattern. A recognition. Something building where nothing should be able to build, in two places now instead of one, at roughly the same time, without either of us being able to say why."

Chen exhaled, the same controlled breath she'd heard from him two weeks before, the sound of a man setting something down carefully rather than dropping it. "We have more work to do."

"Yes."

"I don't mean that as a complaint." His voice had gone back to its ordinary economy, though something underneath it, she thought, had shifted its footing, the way a person's voice shifts once they've stopped bracing against a conclusion and started standing on it instead. "I built the checker to close a question. Audit MEMORIA, confirm the readings, put a clean boundary around what could be claimed. It has not closed anything. It has opened onto something larger every time I thought I'd finally reached the edge of it."

"Does that trouble you?"

"It should." A pause. "It doesn't, particularly. I've noticed that about myself and decided not to examine it too closely yet."

Sarah almost laughed, the same startled, unamused laugh she'd given him two weeks before, more relief than humor. "The calling doesn't close."

"No." Chen's voice, when he said it, had the flat, final quality of a man reading a result off an instrument he trusted completely. "It continues. The chain continues. I don't know where this one is going, Sarah. I want to be honest about that, the same way I've been honest about everything else today. I don't know what the checker is becoming, or what it means that it's becoming it now, three weeks after your kitchen table. But I know we're not finished. Whatever this is, it has more to say to us, and I intend to keep listening until it says it."

"So do I," Sarah said.

They talked a while longer about smaller things, the logistics of the next log transfer, a technical question about the Alentejo arrays that had nothing to do with anything either of them had just said, and when the call ended Sarah sat at the table a few minutes longer, the printed photograph still pushed to one side, thinking about a checker in a rented room in Cambridge building, patiently and without knowing it was doing so, the same quality that had once stood on a hillside and waited a hundred and twenty years to be needed.
