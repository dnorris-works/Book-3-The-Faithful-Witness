
# Chapter 34: Gathering

*The farmhouse, the school, the village. The Alentejo. Over two weeks.*

Jessie did not decide to start paying attention. She simply had been, for a long time, in the way she paid attention to most things that mattered to her: without announcing it, without a starting date she could have named if anyone had asked her to name one, the noticing accumulating the same slow way Leonardo's territorial evidence accumulated, one small fact at a time, filed rather than acted on until enough of them had gathered to make a pattern worth reporting.

At school she was learning the past tense in Portuguese, which the teacher explained by having the class describe what they had done the day before, and Jessie, describing hers, found herself saying that her father had prayed before dinner and her mother had not, a sentence she had not planned to say and that produced no particular reaction from the teacher, who was working on verb endings and not theology, but that Jessie heard herself say and filed, the way she filed most things she heard herself say aloud that she had not entirely meant to.

At home, in the courtyard, she consulted Leonardo on unrelated matters, mostly, though once, crouched by his stone in the late afternoon with Gerald propped beside her for the consultation, she found herself asking him, in the low running commentary she kept up when she was thinking through something, whether lizards noticed when people changed, or whether people only looked the same to lizards the whole time, the same shape and weight and smell, no matter what was different about them underneath. Leonardo did not answer, being a lizard, and Jessie had not expected him to. She had only wanted to say the question out loud somewhere it wouldn't have to go anywhere yet.

She noticed, over those two weeks, that her mother sat differently in church. She could not have said exactly what the difference was if pressed, only that it existed, the way she had known Leonardo had moved before she found the disturbed dirt that proved it: a change registered somewhere beneath the evidence, arriving before the evidence that would confirm it.

She noticed that her mother had started using the good cups on days that weren't Sunday.

She noticed, one evening, that her mother's hand went to her jacket pocket at the kitchen table the way it always did, the small habitual motion Jessie had watched a hundred times without asking about it because it seemed like the kind of thing that belonged to her mother and not to her, the way the cellar belonged to the green lights and not to the governance portfolio Jessie ran with Gerald. This time, though, her mother's hand stayed there a moment longer than usual, and something in her mother's face, briefly, looked like the face Branca had had at the kitchen table the day Jessie asked her whether she'd found it.

Jessie filed this too.

She did not ask her mother what was in the pocket. She had decided, a long time ago, without deciding it formally, that this was the second kind of question, the kind that belonged to someone else the way the cellar belonged to the green lights: hers to notice, not yet hers to open. She had a whole category of these now, gathered over two years the way her record of Leonardo's stones had gathered, a slow accumulation of things observed and set aside, waiting for whatever moment would tell her they were ready to be picked back up.

But the prayer question was different. The prayer question had been gathering the longest of all of them, since the kitchen months ago when she'd first said it out loud to her father and he'd told her he didn't know yet, that he was still thinking about it, and she had accepted that the way she accepted most honest answers that admitted their own limits, filing it as deferred rather than dismissed. She had gone on watching since then, the way she watched everything, without pressure, without needing the answer to arrive on any particular schedule.

What had changed, over these two weeks, was not the question itself. It was the sense, gathering the way pressure gathers before weather, that the question had gotten close enough to being ready that she would recognize the moment when it was time to ask the whole of it out loud, not the small parts she had been asking in pieces for two years, but the real one, the one underneath all the others.

She did not know yet what the real one was, precisely. She only knew it the way she knew most of her most important things: not as a sentence yet, but as a shape, waiting at the edge of her attention for the evening that would finally give it words.

She carried Gerald out to the courtyard one more time before dinner, crouched by Leonardo's stone in the softening light, and told him, in Portuguese because the sentence seemed to want Portuguese more than English, that something was almost ready, though she could not yet say what. Leonardo, characteristically, said nothing back. Jessie did not mind. She had not asked the question to get an answer from him. She had only wanted to hear how it sounded, out loud, before the evening arrived that would ask her to say it to someone who could actually answer.
