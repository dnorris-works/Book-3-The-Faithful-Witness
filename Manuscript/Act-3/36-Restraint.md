
# Chapter 36: Restraint

*The farmhouse. The Alentejo. Late afternoon.*

Figueiredo's car came up the lane at five, the same unhurried crunch of gravel Sarah had learned to recognize without looking, and by the time she reached the gate he was already out of the car, lifting a hand in the same small economical wave he always gave, a folder under his other arm this time instead of a padded envelope.

"Dr. Davidson."

"Sarah's fine. It's been a while."

"A month, I think." He came through the gate at his own unhurried pace, the same restraint he brought to everything, and set the folder on the low wall rather than handing it to her directly, which she had come to understand was its own kind of courtesy, giving her a moment to receive a thing on her own terms rather than his. "The Coimbra museum wanted to know whether you'd had a chance to look at the vestment fragment. I told them I would ask rather than assume."

"MEMORIA answered it two weeks ago. Fifteenth-century, Iberian workshop, dry storage for most of its history. I sent the response through the channel."

"They may not have checked it yet. Museums are slower than collectors." He said this without any particular judgment in it, a simple fact observed the way he observed most facts. "There's also this." He tapped the folder. "A private request from Porto. A woman asking about a set of prayer beads, whether the wear pattern is consistent with the dates she's been given for them."

"I'll take a look tonight."

"No urgency." He glanced past her toward the courtyard, the fig tree, the low wall where Leonardo kept his station, and something in the glance had the same quality it had always had in the two years she'd known him: attentive without being curious, the specific discipline of a man who had trained himself to see a place clearly without asking it to explain itself to him.

"How is the household," he said, which was as close as he ever came to a personal question, and which Sarah had learned to answer with the same proportion he offered it.

"Well. Jessie's learning fast at school. Daniel's writing again, in his own way." She did not mention Branca. She had decided this before he arrived, the same decision she made every time he came, and she watched him not ask, which was its own kind of answer passing between them, wordless and complete.

He did not ask about the woman who had come a month ago and left a week later. He had driven her here himself, had handed Sarah a bag at the gate and said *she is in good hands* and had not, in all the weeks since, asked what had happened once she was inside the gate he'd delivered her through. Sarah had noticed this the first evening and had gone on noticing it every time he came since, the particular shape of his not-asking, which was not the shape of a man who hadn't wondered. She could see, in the careful way he did not look at the room where Branca had slept, that he had wondered plenty. He simply did not let the wondering become a question.

"The channel work is steady," she said, because it seemed like the part of the answer that belonged to him, the part he had actually come to ask about. "The requests keep coming. We keep answering them."

"Good." He nodded, once, the same small economy he brought to everything. "That's the part I can carry back."

She understood, standing there in the last of the afternoon light, exactly what he meant by it, and exactly why he meant it that way. Figueiredo carried messages between people who trusted each other for reasons none of them fully explained to one another, and he carried them faithfully because he had built, over years, a discipline of carrying only what he had actually been given, nothing invented to fill the gaps, nothing assumed to round out a story that wasn't his to complete. What he did not know, he could not give away, not to an intermediary, not under pressure, not by accident in an unguarded sentence to someone who might ask him the wrong question in the wrong tone. His ignorance, where it existed, was not a failure of curiosity. It was the most carefully maintained thing about him, tended the way Daniel tended the fig tree, the way Sarah tended the channel, a discipline practiced daily until it became simply who he was.

"Thank you," she said, "for not asking."

He looked at her for a moment, and something in his face shifted slightly, not surprise exactly, closer to the quiet acknowledgment of a thing correctly named after going a long time unnamed.

"There is very little I need to ask," he said. "I have found, over the years, that the people who send me to places like this one are careful about who they send, and careful about what they let me carry. I trust their carefulness. I have never found a reason not to." He picked the folder back up off the wall, the gesture that meant he was preparing to leave, though he made no move toward the gate yet. "I have another stop before dark."

"You always do."

"It is a small country, but it keeps me busy in it." The nearest thing to a joke she had ever heard him make, delivered with the same flat courtesy he brought to everything else, and she found herself almost smiling in response, the way she sometimes did with Chen, two people meeting each other across a professional distance that had, somewhere along the way, become something warmer without either of them deciding to make it so.

He went back through the gate, lifted his hand once more in the same small wave, and got into the car, and the engine sound faded down the lane the way it always faded, swallowed by the field and the distance, leaving the courtyard to its evening quiet.

Sarah stood a moment longer with the folder in her hand, thinking about a man who had spent years perfecting the specific art of carrying exactly as much as he'd been given and not one thing more, and understood, watching the last of the gravel dust settle on the lane, that his restraint was its own kind of faithfulness, quieter than Branca's, quieter even than her own, but no less complete for being invisible to anyone who did not know to look for it.
