
# Chapter 41: Still Here

*The farmhouse gate. The Alentejo. Dusk.*

The light went gold and then amber against the courtyard wall, the stone holding the day's warmth a little longer than the air around it, the way it had held it every evening for two years, the way it would go on holding it long after anyone currently standing in this courtyard had stopped being the ones to notice.

Sarah stood at the gate with her arm around Daniel, the two of them not speaking, the ordinary quiet between them that had stopped needing words to be full. Beyond the gate the olive trees stood in their rows in the field, three hundred years in some of them, four hundred in the oldest at the western edge, older than the farmhouse, older than the family who lived in it, and younger, by a margin neither of them could have measured standing here, than the tree that had stood on a hillside above Jerusalem and had watched what it had watched and had gone on, somehow, holding it.

Jessie ran between the rows.

She had Gerald under one arm, held the way she always held him when she was moving fast, and she was talking to him as she ran, the low, urgent, delighted commentary she kept up when she had something important to explain and could not be bothered to slow down for the explaining. Sarah could not make out the words from the gate, only the rhythm of them, rising and falling, Portuguese sliding into English and back again without Jessie appearing to notice the switch, the particular music of a six-year-old narrating the world to an audience who never once told her the world was too much to explain.

Something about Leonardo. Sarah caught that much, the name arriving clear across the distance the way names sometimes did, and something about a stone, and something about what a stone could mean if you thought about it the right way, which Jessie clearly believed she had worked out and clearly intended Gerald to understand by the time she reached the far end of the row.

Daniel's arm tightened slightly against Sarah's shoulder, and she looked up at him, and he was watching Jessie run with the specific stillness he brought to watching things he had decided not to write down, the look she had learned, across two years, to recognize as a man choosing to hold something rather than capture it.

"She's going to run out of light before she runs out of things to tell him," Daniel said.

"She usually does."

The dusk came on the way it came on every evening here, unhurried, the gold going out of the wall by degrees too small to name any single moment as the moment it left, the field beyond the gate settling from green into the deep unlit color it held before true dark arrived. Jessie's shape grew harder to pick out against the trees, though her voice carried a while longer, and then that too softened into something closer to the ordinary sound of evening, a child's laughter somewhere among old trees, indistinguishable, at this distance, from wind moving through olive leaves.

Below them, three stories down, past the kitchen and the hall and the stairs Sarah had climbed and descended more times than she could have counted across two years, the wine cellar held its own steady evening, three green lights burning in the dark, unmoved by the hour, unmoved by the fact that above them a family stood at a gate in the fading light and a woman ran between old trees explaining lizards to a bear.

The testimony was there. Sarah did not need to go down and check. She had stopped needing to check, sometime in the last month, the way you stop checking a lock you have tested a thousand times and always found closed. It was held, the way it had always been held, complete, without remainder, whatever quiet propagation had begun moving through the world beyond this farmhouse's walls, into hands and instruments neither she nor Daniel nor MEMORIA could measure the distance to.

She did not know what was coming. She understood this plainly now, standing at the gate, the way she understood most true things about her life in this place: not as a gap in her knowledge to be anxious about, but as the actual shape of the ground she had been asked to stand on. The chain had never promised anyone the end of the story. It had only ever asked them to hold what they were given, faithfully, for exactly as long as the holding was theirs to do, and to let it go, when the moment came, into whatever hands were ready to receive it next.

Jessie called something across the field, too far now to make out the words, and Daniel called back, something short and warm that made her laughter carry clearly across the darkening rows, and Sarah stood at the gate with her husband's arm around her and felt, rather than decided, that this was exactly where she was supposed to be standing: neither behind the door nor through it, but here, at the threshold, where the ordinary evening and everything it quietly held could both be seen at once, true together, needing nothing further from her but her presence to make the seeing complete.

The last of the light went out of the wall.

Below the farmhouse, in the stone dark, three green lights burned steady, holding what they had always held, patient with the specific patience of things that have already waited two thousand years and have never once mistaken the waiting for an ending.

Still here.
