
# Chapter 32: Clarity

*The farmhouse and the village church. The Alentejo. Morning.*

Two weeks after the gate had closed behind Figueiredo's car, Sarah's mornings had gone back to looking exactly like the mornings that had come before Branca, which she noticed the way she noticed most things about herself, a little late, standing at the kitchen counter at six with the kettle not yet boiled and the light coming through the window at the same pale early angle it always came through at this hour.

She opened the channel on her laptop. Two requests overnight, a small one from a private collector in Faro about a coin's patina, a longer one from a museum in Coimbra asking about a fragment of liturgical vestment. She read them the way she read everything now, carefully, without hurrying, and answered the coin herself, the patina question simple enough after two years that she no longer needed MEMORIA for it, and set the vestment fragment aside to bring downstairs later in the morning.

Jessie came in for breakfast with Gerald under one arm, already narrating some development involving Leonardo that did not, this time, require formal governance review, and Daniel made the eggs, and the kitchen held its ordinary shape around all of it, the shape it had held for two years, the shape it had held even during the week it had also held Branca.

Nothing about the morning was different.

And yet she moved through it aware of something in herself that had not been there in quite the same way six months before, a settledness she did not have a good word for and had stopped trying to force one onto. Not a change. She was careful with herself about that distinction, the same care she brought to dating an object she wasn't ready to date. A change implied that something had been one thing and had become another, and she did not feel like a different woman standing at this counter than she had been a year ago. She felt, instead, like the same woman seeing more clearly what had already been true. Water clarifying. Light clarifying, the haze burning off a field in the hour after dawn, revealing a shape that had been there the whole time, only obscured.

She carried the vestment fragment down to the cellar mid-morning and read the request aloud to MEMORIA, and MEMORIA answered plainly, the weave consistent with a fifteenth-century Iberian workshop, the gold thread's degradation pattern narrowing the storage conditions to somewhere dry and dark for most of its history, and Sarah typed the answer into the channel and sent it and did not linger, because there was nothing tonight that needed lingering over, only the ordinary business of the morning continuing to be ordinary.

On Sunday she went with Daniel and Jessie to Mass in the village.

She had done this before, a handful of times over two years, always with the same careful accounting of it to herself afterward: she went because Jessie liked to go with her father, because the walk through the village in the cool part of the morning was its own small pleasure, because sitting in an old stone building for an hour asked nothing of her that she was not willing to give. She had never once, in two years, thought of it as anything more than accompaniment.

She sat in the pew between Jessie and the aisle, Daniel on Jessie's other side, and the priest's Portuguese moved past her the way it always moved past her, familiar in its shape though she caught less than half its content, the cadence of a liturgy she had grown up inside a different version of, the same bones under a different skin. The light came through the high windows at an angle that put a long pale bar across the stone floor, moving almost imperceptibly as the hour went on, the way it always moved in a church at that time of morning, marking time without anyone needing to check a clock.

She did not go forward when the line formed for communion. She had not expected to, had not, in fact, considered it at all until the moment the line was actually forming and she found herself watching Daniel rise with Jessie's hand in his and thinking, plainly, without any of the old defensiveness she used to bring to the thought: not yet.

Not never. She noticed the distinction the moment it arrived and sat with it, the pale bar of light lying across the floor a few feet from her shoes, the low murmur of the line moving forward and back. She had spent years assuming that sitting in the pew while others went forward was a kind of failure, a marker of the distance she had never closed. She understood now, watching Daniel kneel at the rail with their daughter beside him, that she was not failing to close a distance. She was standing exactly where she had always stood, at the place in the room where both the going-forward and the staying-seated could be seen clearly and neither one canceled the other out.

She was not outside the building. She had not been outside it for longer than she'd let herself notice. She was at the door of it, in the specific sense a door means something more than a wall, and she sat there through the rest of the Mass in a stillness that had nothing performed in it and nothing withheld either, only the plain fact of a woman occupying the threshold she had built, without deciding to build it, across two years of carrying a piece of wood in her pocket and answering, honestly, whatever questions arrived at her door.

Afterward, walking back through the village in the full sun, Jessie ran ahead with Gerald to look at a dog someone had tied outside the bakery, and Daniel fell into step beside Sarah without saying anything, and she found she did not need him to say anything, because whatever he had seen in the pew, he was carrying it the same unhurried way she was carrying it herself, and the carrying, this time, did not feel like something either of them needed to set down before they got home.
