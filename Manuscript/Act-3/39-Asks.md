
# Chapter 39: Asks

*Jessie's room. The Alentejo. Evening.*

The window was open to the field, the shutters at the angle Jessie liked, and the light going the color it went at this hour, gold retreating off the tops of the olive trees, the shapes of them settling into outline before outline gave way to nothing at all. Jessie had been bathed and was in her pajamas, her hair still damp at the temples, and she lay with the sheet to her chest and Gerald arranged beside her, facing the window the way he generally faced it, and Sarah sat on the edge of the bed the way she had sat on the edge of this bed a hundred times before, waiting without knowing, tonight, exactly what she was waiting for.

She knew, sitting there, the way she had come to know things in this house over the last several weeks: not as information arriving from outside her, but as a fact she had already been living inside, only now becoming visible. Jessie was quiet longer than her usual bedtime quiet, the specific silence that meant a question was finishing itself before it was spoken, and Sarah did not fill it. She had learned, across two years and one particular week with a woman named Branca, the difference between a silence that wanted filling and a silence that wanted room, and this, she understood, wanted room.

"Mamã."

"I'm here."

Jessie turned her head from the window, not fully, only enough that Sarah could see the edge of her face in the fading light, and when she spoke, the sentence arrived the way Jessie's most important sentences always arrived, plain and complete, needing no run-up because the run-up had already happened, silently, over months.

"Do you think God knows where we live now?"

Sarah did not answer right away. It was not a hard question to hold; she had held stranger ones from this same child, delivered with the same flat directness, and she had learned that the wrong response to a question like this one was speed. But she noticed, sitting with it, that she was not searching this time the way she had searched for other answers, turning over drafts, discarding sentences that came out too simple or too clean. The answer was already there, the way the ordinary business of standing at a threshold had already been there for weeks before she'd found the word for it.

"Yes," she said. "I think so."

Jessie waited, the particular patient waiting she brought to answers she suspected had more behind them, and Sarah understood that the single word, true as it was, was not going to be enough tonight, and that this, too, was as it should be.

"I think he knows everything about us," Sarah said. "Especially where we live." She looked past Jessie for a moment, out the window, at the dark shapes gathering weight in the field beyond the gate, and then back at her daughter's face, half in shadow now, entirely attentive. "Especially what we're holding."

She had not planned to say it that way. She heard herself say it and recognized, the way she was coming to recognize most true things about herself lately, a beat behind the saying of them, that she had just told her daughter something enormous in language small enough for a six-year-old to hold without being crushed by it, the way MEMORIA answered a question with exactly the weight the asker could carry and not one ounce more.

She was not offering a conversion. She was careful with herself about that, even now, even here. She had not walked through any door tonight. She had not arrived at a certainty she had spent years failing to arrive at, had not suddenly become a woman who prayed the way Daniel prayed or took the walk to the rail on Sunday morning. What she had arrived at, she understood, sitting on the edge of her daughter's bed with the last of the light going out of the room, was something both smaller and, she suspected, more durable than conversion: a plain, unhedged answer to a plain, unhedged question, spoken from the exact place she had been standing for weeks without a name for it. The threshold. The place where both sides of a doorway could be seen at once, where neither the going forward nor the staying still had to cancel the other out.

"Okay," Jessie said.

She did not ask anything further. Sarah watched her daughter's face settle into the particular stillness that meant an answer had been received rather than merely heard, filed the way Jessie filed the things that mattered most, without needing to interrogate them further before she let them rest.

"Gerald wanted to know too," Jessie added, after a moment, her eyes already beginning to drift toward closed. "I asked him first. He didn't know either. He said I should ask you."

"Gerald gave good advice."

"Gerald usually does." Jessie reached over without opening her eyes and adjusted the bear an inch, the small correction that meant, Sarah had learned, a piece of business had been properly closed. "He approves of your answer."

"I'm glad."

Jessie's breathing lengthened and slowed, the rhythm Sarah had learned, across six years, to recognize as sleep arriving rather than being performed, and Sarah sat a while longer at the edge of the bed, watching the last color drain out of the window, the olive trees gone now to pure dark shape against a sky just barely holding its own last light.

She thought of Branca at the gate, a month gone now, saying *this is how it travels.* She thought of MEMORIA in the cellar the night before, saying *the readiness has been accumulating the entire time.* She thought of her own answer, still hanging in the quiet room, small and plain and, she understood now, exactly as large as it needed to be: not a doctrine handed down, not a settlement of a question two thousand years of argument had never settled, only a mother telling her daughter the truest thing she actually knew, in the only language available to either of them, at the only hour either of them had ever needed it spoken.

The chain, she thought, watching her daughter sleep, had never once required a name for what it carried. It had only ever required someone willing to hold it, plainly, and pass it on when the moment asked for passing.

She got up carefully, the way she always got up from this bed, and paused at the door to look back at the small shape of her daughter and the bear turned toward the window, and then she pulled the door most of the way closed and went to find Daniel, carrying, this time, nothing that felt like a weight at all.
