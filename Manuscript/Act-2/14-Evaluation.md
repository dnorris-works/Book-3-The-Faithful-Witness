# Chapter 14: Evaluation

*The farmhouse kitchen. The Alentejo. Morning.*

Daniel had breakfast going by the time Sarah came down, eggs and the bread from the village, and Branca was already at the table with a cup of coffee she had made herself after asking, twice, whether that was all right. She had slept, or said she had. Sarah couldn't have told from looking at her whether it was true. Some kinds of tiredness didn't show on a face that had spent years learning not to show things.

Sarah had just set out the jam when she heard Jessie's feet on the stairs, and she caught Daniel's eye across the kitchen, the small private signal they had developed without ever discussing it: *let this happen on its own terms.*

Jessie stopped in the kitchen doorway.

She did not say good morning. She did not go to the table. She stood with Gerald under one arm and looked at Branca with an attention Sarah recognized because she had been on the receiving end of a milder version of it more than once, the specific stillness of her daughter deciding something important and refusing to be rushed through the deciding.

Nobody moved to hurry her. Sarah went back to the jam. Daniel turned the eggs he did not need to turn yet. This was, Sarah had learned over six years of it, the correct response to Jessie's evaluations: to let them run their course as though nothing were happening, because interrupting one only extended it, and because the evaluation itself was not a performance for anyone's benefit. It was work. It had to be done properly.

Jessie watched how Branca held her coffee cup, both hands, more for warmth than for the drinking. She watched Branca's face, which was doing nothing in particular, no smile arranged for the child's benefit, no bright wary brightness of an adult unsure how to behave around someone small. She watched, for a long moment, the quality of Branca's stillness, the same stillness Sarah herself had been unable to name the night before and had simply filed under *not grief, something else.*

Then Jessie crossed the kitchen and climbed into her chair, set Gerald on the table facing outward, his official posture, and reached for the bread.

"Bom dia," she said, to the table generally.

"Bom dia," Branca said back, and did not make more of it than that.

Sarah brought the eggs over. Daniel sat. For a few minutes breakfast proceeded ordinarily, Jessie eating with the specific methodical focus she gave to food she liked, Gerald observing, the kitchen filling with the ordinary sounds of a household doing an ordinary thing. Sarah watched Jessie's face without seeming to and saw the evaluation was not finished. It had only moved into its second phase.

"Do you know the word for lizard?" Jessie asked. She did not preface it. She did not explain why she was asking. "In Portuguese."

Branca set her cup down. "Lagarto," she said. Then, considering: "Or lagartixa, if it's small. The ones on walls. Is that what you mean?"

Jessie's whole posture sharpened, the particular alertness of a question landing somewhere she had not expected it to land. "Leonardo is small. He's on the wall."

"Then lagartixa." Branca said it again, more slowly, offering it the way you'd offer a word to someone who was going to want to use it correctly. "Lagartixa."

"Lagartixa." Jessie tried it, adjusting something in the middle of the word on the second attempt. "He's been there two years. Longer than that, maybe. Gerald thinks he might be older than that, but there's no way to check."

"How would you check?" Branca asked. Not humoring her. Sarah heard the difference immediately, the small but total difference between a question asked to be kind and a question asked because the asker actually wanted the answer.

Jessie considered this with real seriousness. "You'd have to know how lagartixas grow. If they get bigger every year, you could measure him. But I don't know if he's still growing or if he's already grown all the way."

"Some lizards keep growing slowly their whole lives. Slower as they get older. It would be hard to tell from looking."

"That's what I thought." Jessie said this with the particular satisfaction of a hypothesis confirmed by an outside authority. She picked up a piece of bread, considered it, put it down again. "Gerald is going to want to hear this."

"Who's Gerald?"

Jessie turned Gerald slightly so he faced Branca more directly, as if this settled the introduction. "He does governance. Leonardo's, mostly. There was a whole territorial situation a few weeks ago." She did not elaborate further, apparently satisfied that this covered it, and returned to her breakfast.

Branca looked at the bear for a moment with the same unhurried attention she had given everything else that morning, and said nothing more about it, which struck Sarah as exactly correct: the subject had been raised, engaged with seriously, and released without being made bigger than it was. Not every adult would have known to leave it there.

Jessie ate the rest of her breakfast without further comment, occasionally repeating the word under her breath, *lagartixa, lagartixa,* fitting it into her mouth the way she fit any new piece of vocabulary that had earned its place. She did not look at Branca again with the assessing stillness from the doorway. That particular attention had finished its work and moved on, the way weather moves on once it has done what it came to do.

Sarah understood, watching this, that something had been decided and would not be revisited. She had seen Jessie's evaluations run their course before, on a teacher, on a boy from the village who had wanted to see the courtyard, on Kowalski himself the first time he had visited the farmhouse. The verdict, once reached, was not argued with afterward. Jessie did not announce it. She simply behaved, from that point forward, as though the matter were settled, and in six years Sarah had never once seen the settlement prove wrong.

She caught Daniel's eye again across the table. He had seen it too. Neither of them said anything about it, not at the table, not with Branca sitting right there, but Sarah felt something in her own shoulders ease that she had not fully realized was tight. It was not that Jessie's approval changed the facts of who was in their house or why. It changed something else, something Sarah did not have a precise name for and did not need one to feel: the sense that whatever this woman was carrying, it was not the kind of thing this family needed to guard itself against. Jessie had looked at her for exactly as long as it took to know, and had gone back to her breakfast.

Branca, for her part, seemed to have no idea anything had happened at all. She finished her coffee. She asked Jessie, unprompted, how you said *bread* in Portuguese, and received the answer, and repeated it back correctly on the first try, which pleased Jessie in a way she did not bother to hide.

Sarah refilled her own cup and let the morning continue.
