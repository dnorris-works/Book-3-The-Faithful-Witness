# Chapter 19: Alone

*The farmhouse. The Alentejo. Evening.*

Branca sat on the edge of the bed with her hands in her lap and did not move for a long time.

The room was simple, exactly as Sarah had said it would be, and simple suited her. A bed, a chair, a small table with a lamp she had not turned on yet, the last of the evening light still doing the work instead. She had unpacked almost nothing. There had been almost nothing to unpack. A change of clothes. A washbag. The paper with her own name on it in Kowalski's handwriting, which she had folded back into her coat pocket rather than leaving on the table, because some things did not belong on a table where anyone might see them and ask.

And the other thing, at the bottom of the bag, wrapped in a handkerchief that had been her mother's before it was hers.

She did not take it out. She knew exactly where it was without looking, the same certainty you have about a bruise on your own body, needing no pressure to confirm it is still there. Father Kowalski had pressed it into her hand the morning she left, had not explained himself, had simply said, *take this, it isn't much, but it is what I have,* and she had not asked what he meant because she had learned, across three years of his direction, that Kowalski's gifts were rarely about the object itself.

She had carried it for two weeks now, from Rome to the train, from the train to Figueiredo's car, from the car to this room, and she had not once opened the handkerchief to look at it. She did not need to look at it. She had felt its shape through the cloth on the first day and had known immediately what it was, and since then it had simply been present in the bag, a known fact, unexamined, load-bearing.

She thought about the years before this one, not in order, not as a story, but in the loose, undefended fashion memory arrives when a person is tired: a hallway that smelled of antiseptic and, underneath it, something warmer that no cleaning product ever fully erased. A chair pulled as close to a bed as the equipment allowed. Her own hand, steady, because steadiness was the one thing she had learned to give without fail, the one gift she had never once failed to deliver even when everything underneath the steadiness was breaking.

She had stayed. She always stayed. It was the thing she was known for, the thing the night shift asked for by name when a case was going to be hard. She had sat through hundreds of endings and had never once left early, never once found a reason to be elsewhere, unlike some of her colleagues, decent people, who found reasons to be elsewhere when a room got too heavy to hold. She had held it. Every time. She had believed, without ever quite deciding to believe it, that the holding meant something, that presence itself was a kind of gift a person could give even when nothing else was left to give.

And then it had been her own turn to sit in that chair, and she had stayed exactly as she had always stayed, and it had made no difference at all.

She did not let herself finish that thought all the way to its edges, not tonight, not in a strange room in a country that was still her own but did not yet feel like anywhere. She had learned to stop the thought partway, the same discipline that keeps a person from pressing on a wound that has not finished healing, not because the healing was progressing but because pressing further only confirmed what she already knew: that it still hurt, and that knowing this changed nothing.

She stood, finally, when the light in the room had gone from gold to the deeper blue that came just before true dark, and crossed to the window.

The courtyard was almost sourceless in this light, shapes rather than objects: the fig tree a darker mass against a dark wall, the low stone where the child had told her a lizard lived, the gate standing open a hand's width, as it seemed to stand most evenings, as though nobody in this house believed very strongly in keeping anything out. Beyond the gate the olive field ran back toward a horizon she could no longer distinguish from the sky, and the whole landscape held the particular stillness of somewhere that had been quiet for a very long time and had decided, somewhere in its history, that quiet suited it.

She stood at the window and breathed.

There was something in the air here she did not have a word for, something she had noticed on the drive in and had assumed, at the time, was simply the unfamiliarity of a new place working on her senses. It had not faded. If anything it had sharpened, this evening, standing here with the room behind her going dark and the handkerchief and its contents sitting at the bottom of a bag she had not touched since arriving. It was not peace exactly. She was suspicious of that word; she had heard it offered to her too many times by people who meant well and had never sat in the chair. It was something more like attention. As though the ground itself, the stones, the old trees standing in their rows, had been paying a very long and very patient kind of attention to something, and had not yet finished, and did not mind that she had walked into the middle of it without knowing what it was.

She did not know why she had come here. She had told the child this morning, plainly, because the child had asked plainly and had earned a plain answer: *I needed to come.* It had felt true when she said it and it felt true now, standing at a window in the dark, and she still could not have said what the sentence meant beyond its own bare shape.

She thought, briefly, of Kowalski's face the morning he had given her the handkerchief, the particular carefulness in it, a man who had run out of the things he knew how to offer and was handing her toward something he could not name and did not fully trust himself to describe. He had not promised her an answer. He had promised her only that these people were safe, and that she would not be asked to be anything she was not.

She did not know yet whether that was enough. She stood at the window a while longer, watching the dark come down fully over the olive trees, and let the not-knowing sit in her, unresolved, the same discipline eleven years at other people's bedsides had taught her: let unanswerable things sit without forcing them toward a shape they were not ready to take.

After a while she turned from the window, and did not open the bag, and lay down on top of the covers in her clothes, and watched the ceiling until sleep came for her without her quite noticing the moment it arrived.
