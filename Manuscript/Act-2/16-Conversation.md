# Chapter 16: Conversation

*The farmhouse courtyard. The Alentejo. Afternoon.*

Sarah found Branca on the courtyard bench a little after two, the one she and Daniel used in the evenings, angled so that whoever sat on it faced the fig tree and the low wall rather than the house. Branca had not chosen the angle for any particular reason, Sarah guessed. It was simply where the shade fell at that hour, and Branca struck her as someone who took the shade where it was offered rather than arranging the day around her own preferences.

"May I."

Branca moved her bag to make room, though there had been room already. "Of course."

They sat for a moment without speaking. Somewhere past the wall a dog was barking at something and then stopped, and the courtyard settled back into the particular quiet of early afternoon, cicadas holding steady in the field beyond the gate.

"How was the journey," Sarah said. It was not quite a question and not quite small talk, and she let it sit in the space between the two.

"Long. Trains, then Figueiredo's car for the last part." Branca considered this further before adding anything else, a habit, it seemed, that governed most of what she said. "The light changes as you come south. I noticed it somewhere after Évora. Everything went the color it is now."

"It does that. Takes people by surprise the first year, usually. Then you stop noticing you noticed it."

"I can imagine that." Branca looked at the fig tree for a while. "It's a generous kind of light."

Sarah did not answer that, not because there was nothing to say but because it seemed like the kind of sentence that did not need an answer, only a witness. She let it stand. Branca did not seem to expect anything different. They sat another minute in the same silence, and Sarah found she did not mind it. Silence with people she did not yet know usually carried some obligation to fill it. This one did not ask to be filled.

"Was the trip difficult otherwise?" Sarah asked eventually. "Kowalski didn't tell us much about how it was arranged."

"He arranged very little, on purpose, I think." Branca's hands were folded in her lap, still, the same economy of movement Sarah had noticed the evening before. "A train ticket. Figueiredo's number. Your names." A small pause. "He is careful about what he gives people. I used to think it was caution. I've started to think it's something closer to respect."

"For what?"

"For the size of the thing he's not saying." Branca said this lightly, almost casually, and then let another silence follow it, one that felt different from the ones before, less like courtesy and more like something being set down on the bench between them.

Sarah waited.

"He told me you keep something here," Branca said. She did not say it as a question. She said it the way she had said everything else so far, plainly, without performance, a fact offered rather than a door being tested. "He didn't say what. I don't think he entirely knows. But he said it the way you'd say something about a person's character, not their possessions. As if it were the truest thing about you he could tell me without telling me anything at all."

Sarah felt something settle in her chest that was not quite alarm and not quite relief, some third thing she did not have a ready name for. Two years of channel work had taught her to recognize the exact moment when a requester's question changed register, when someone stopped asking what an object was and started asking what it meant, and she recognized that shift now, arriving not from an object but from the woman sitting beside her.

"He's not wrong," Sarah said. It was the only sentence she could find that was both true and safe to give.

Branca nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something she had already decided rather than told her anything new. "I didn't expect you to say more than that."

"Would it help if I did?"

"I don't know yet." Branca looked at her directly for the first time since they'd sat down, and there was nothing demanding in it, no request dressed up as curiosity. "I don't think I came here for an explanation. Father Kowalski has given me a great many of those over the years. None of them have reached the thing I actually need reached."

"What does reach it?"

"I don't know that either. That's rather the trouble." A faint, unpracticed smile, gone almost before it arrived. "I've been a professional witness to dying for eleven years. I know how to sit with people in the hardest hour of their lives and give them exactly what they need without being asked. I have never once known how to do that for myself."

Sarah sat with that the same way she sat with an unresolved reading, refusing to reach for a conclusion before the evidence had finished arriving. She did not offer comfort. Something in the set of Branca's shoulders told her comfort was not what was being asked for, and she had learned, across two years in a cellar with green lights, the difference between a question that wanted an answer and a question that only wanted to be heard existing.

"I don't know what you're carrying," Sarah said. "Kowalski didn't tell us that either. But you're welcome here regardless of what it turns out to be."

"I believe that." Branca said it simply, without embellishment, and turned back toward the fig tree. "It's a strange thing, to believe something before you understand why it's true."

"Yes," Sarah said, and found she meant it more fully than the single word suggested.

They sat a while longer without speaking, both of them looking at the same tree, the same wall, the same ordinary afternoon light, and something had shifted in the space between them that neither named and neither needed to. Sarah understood, without being able to point to the exact sentence that had done it, that they had stopped being hostess and guest sometime in the last quarter hour, and had become, instead, two people who had recognized something true in each other and had chosen, for now, to let it rest there, unexplained, between them on the bench.
