
# Chapter 28: Carry

*Jessie's room. The Alentejo. Night.*

The window was open to the field, the shutters at the angle Jessie liked, and the room held the particular hush it held every night around eight, the day's noise settled into the walls the way heat settled into the courtyard stone. Sarah sat on the edge of the bed. Jessie lay with the sheet to her chest and Gerald arranged beside her, facing the window as he generally did, and she had the look Sarah had learned to recognize over six years, the specific stillness of a child assembling a question she'd been carrying since some point earlier in the day and had decided, now, in the dark, was finally the right time to ask it.

"Where did Branca go," Jessie said.

"Home. Back to where she lives, near Lisbon."

Jessie considered this. "Why did she come here in the first place?"

Sarah had thought about how she might answer this if it were asked, in the two days since the gate, and found, now that the moment had actually arrived, that the answer she had prepared felt too complicated for the room. She tried the simpler one instead, the one that was also, she thought, the truer one.

"She needed something," Sarah said. "I don't think she could have told you what it was, not in a way that would have made sense out loud. But she needed it, and someone who knew that sent her to us, because he thought we might have it."

"Did you?"

"I think so. I think she found it."

Jessie turned this over the way she turned most things, without hurrying it, her eyes on the ceiling rather than on Sarah, which was often how she thought best. "Will she come back?"

"I don't know. Maybe. It isn't the kind of thing you can plan."

"Okay." Jessie said it plainly, the way she accepted most answers that were honest about their own limits, and Sarah understood, watching her, that the not-knowing had not troubled her daughter any more than it had troubled Sarah herself, standing at the gate two mornings ago.

"She has it now," Sarah said, because it seemed like the part of the answer that mattered most, even if she wasn't certain Jessie needed it spelled out. "What she needed. She found it, and now she carries it with her, wherever she goes. It doesn't stay behind just because she leaves the place she found it."

Jessie was quiet for a while, long enough that Sarah thought she might have drifted, the way she sometimes did mid-conversation, sliding into sleep between one thought and the next without any particular ceremony about it. Then she spoke again, and her voice had the specific weight it carried when she had connected two things she had been holding separately and had only just noticed they belonged together.

"The olive trees carry things too, don't they."

Sarah looked at her. "What do you mean?"

"Not like Branca. Different things." Jessie was still looking at the ceiling, working it out as she said it. "But they've been there a long time. Longer than us. They probably remember things the way the tree in Papá's stories remembers things. Just not as much, because they're not as old."

Sarah felt something settle in her chest, the particular quiet recognition she had felt once before in this exact room, at this exact hour, the night she had told Jessie the tree in the stories was older than the city they'd lived in before. She had thought, that night, that she was simply answering a bedtime question. She understood now, the way she was coming to understand most of what happened in this house, late and all at once, that her daughter had been quietly building the same understanding in her own way, from her own materials, without anyone handing it to her directly.

"Yes," Sarah said. "I think that's exactly right."

Jessie nodded, satisfied, the particular small nod that meant a fact had been confirmed rather than newly given, and Sarah wondered, not for the first time, how much of what she had assumed she was protecting her daughter from had simply been absorbed instead, quietly, the way soil takes up water without announcing that it has done so.

"Everything carries something," Jessie said, half into the pillow now, her eyes beginning to close. "Even if you can't see it."

"Even if you can't see it," Sarah agreed.

Jessie reached over without opening her eyes and adjusted Gerald an inch, the small correction she made before she would allow herself to fully drift, and then her breathing lengthened and slowed into the rhythm Sarah had learned, across six years, to recognize as sleep rather than its imitation.

Sarah sat a few minutes longer, the window open to the dark shapes of the olive trees standing in their rows beyond the gate, holding whatever they held in whatever manner trees held things, patient and wordless and entirely unconcerned with being understood. She thought about Branca somewhere north of here tonight, in a life Sarah knew almost nothing about, carrying something that had not been visible when she arrived and had not become visible when she left, only settled, the way a fact settles once it no longer needs proving.

She got up carefully, the way she always got up from this bed, and paused at the door to look back once at the small shape of her daughter and the bear angled toward the window, and then she pulled the door most of the way closed and went to find Daniel, carrying, herself, the particular unshowable weight of an evening that had asked nothing more of her than to sit still and answer honestly.
