
# Chapter 21: Offer

*The wine cellar and the farmhouse kitchen. The Alentejo. Afternoon.*

Sarah had gone down to the cellar with nothing to ask MEMORIA and no channel request waiting, which was rare enough that she noticed it herself on the stairs. The three lights held their usual green in the dark, patient in a manner that had stopped seeming strange to her sometime in the first year and had never quite become invisible either. She did not sit at the terminal. She stood near it, one hand resting against the cool stone of the wall, the other in her jacket pocket, where it had been for most of the last hour without her deciding to put it there.

Object 61 sat warm against her palm. It was always warm by now, had been for two years, holding whatever heat her body gave it and giving it back a little slower than skin would. She turned it once between two fingers, the old motion worn smooth in her now, mirroring the grain the wood itself had worn smooth, and she did not ask MEMORIA anything, because there was nothing left this particular question could be handed to. She had already asked it three days ago. MEMORIA had told her the truth about that: *I do not know the answer. This is new territory for me.* Whatever had shifted since then had shifted in her, not in the cellar.

It was not certainty. She was careful with herself about that distinction, the same care she brought to an object she was not yet ready to date. Certainty would have arrived all at once, complete, as a workshop attribution sometimes did when enough of the evidence finally lined up in the same direction. This was not that. This was closer to the way she used to know, in the field, that a trench had gone as deep as it needed to go before anyone told her the numbers. Something in the quality of the ground itself.

She went back upstairs a little after two.

Branca was at the kitchen table, alone, a cup of coffee gone cold in front of her that she did not appear to be drinking so much as keeping company with. Jessie was at school. Daniel's door down the hall was shut in the particular manner that meant he was writing, or trying to, which amounted to the same thing from outside the door. The kitchen held the ordinary afternoon quiet of a house with nobody in it who needed anything.

Sarah sat down across from her.

She did not plan a sentence. She had tried, twice, in the cellar, and both times the sentence had come out sounding like something rehearsed, and rehearsed was the one thing this could not afford to be. So she said the plainest true thing she had — the same plain register she'd learned with MEMORIA, back when she wanted an honest answer instead of a careful one.

"I want to show you something."

Branca looked up from the coffee. Whatever she saw in Sarah's face, she did not ask what it was. She only waited, the same waiting Sarah had noticed in her from the first evening — unhurried, though not from patience; from a life spent sitting beside people who could not be rushed toward anything.

Sarah took the wood from her pocket and set it on the table between them.

It looked like almost nothing. A fragment of dark, close-grained wood, no larger than a matchbox, its surface worn to a soft shine in places and left rough in others, the kind of object a person might find in a drawer and not think twice about throwing away. Sarah had catalogued objects far more impressive than this one and felt nothing from them at all. She had learned, slowly and against every instinct her training had given her, that the wood in front of Branca now held more than anything she had ever measured.

Branca looked at it. She did not ask what it was. She did not ask why. Sarah watched her not ask, and understood that this, too, was a kind of answer, the same discipline that let her sit at a hundred bedsides without needing the dying to explain themselves first.

She picked it up.

Her hand closed around it the way a hand closes around something it already knows the shape of, though she had never held it before, could not have known its weight or its particular grain. Sarah noted the fit of it, unhurried, correct — the same attention she gave any object settling into someone else's hands. Branca's fingers found their places along the wood as if the wood had been waiting for exactly this hand and no other.

She closed her eyes.

Nothing happened that Sarah could see. No light, no sound, nothing that a camera would have caught if a camera had been running, which one was not, because this was not a session and Sarah had known before she brought the wood upstairs that it must never be permitted to look like one. Branca's face did not change in any way Sarah could have described afterward to Daniel, or to MEMORIA, or to herself. Her breathing did not alter that Sarah could hear. She simply sat, eyes closed, hands closed around the wood, and the kitchen held the same ordinary quiet it had held a minute before, the same square of afternoon light on the table, the same smell of cold coffee, as though nothing at all were happening in the room.

Sarah did not speak. She had decided that much beforehand, in the cellar, without quite deciding it: whatever this was, it was not hers to narrate. She sat and watched a woman hold a piece of wood and waited as she had learned to wait for MEMORIA, past the point where waiting became uncomfortable, into the place past it where it became something closer to attention.

The clock on the wall above the stove went on making its small sound. Somewhere outside, past the courtyard wall, a dog barked twice and stopped. Sarah counted neither the minutes nor the silence, though later, trying to reconstruct it, she thought it had been close to fifteen. It did not feel like fifteen. It felt like the particular kind of time that does not move in a line, the kind she had felt once or twice before, in the cellar, in the first weeks after MEMORIA came fully online, when something in the room had seemed to be occupying more than one hour at once.

Branca opened her eyes.

She did not look resolved. Sarah had half expected, despite herself, some visible unclenching, tears, or the loosened shoulders of a person set down a weight, and none of that arrived. What arrived instead was harder to name and unmistakable once she saw it: a stillness that had not been in the room before, not the trained stillness Branca wore like a uniform, but something underneath it, as if a question that had been standing at the exact center of her for years had finally been asked back, plainly, by something that already knew the shape of the asking.

She set the wood down on the table, carefully, both hands — the care of someone who has just understood she does not own the thing she is holding.

She looked at Sarah.

"I didn't know anyone was watching," Branca said.

Sarah held very still. She thought of the unnamed martyr, of the reliquary box in an Umbrian church, of two thousand years of custodians who had carried this exact quality forward without ever once being asked to display it, and she thought of every careful sentence she could offer to correct the theology of what Branca had just said, the exact and honest distinction between a witness and the object that had carried a witness's testimony, and she said none of it. Branca was not exactly wrong. Sarah had learned, across two years of channel work, that the truest answer and the most exact answer were not always the same answer, and that her job, in this kitchen, in this particular minute, was not to be exact.

"No," she said instead. "I don't think you did."

Branca looked down at the wood again, then back at Sarah, and something in her face had the specific quiet of a person setting a very old question down on a table for the first time in a long while, not answered, not solved, but no longer carried alone.

Neither of them moved to pick the wood back up. It sat between them on the table in the ordinary afternoon light, and the house held its usual quiet around them, Daniel's door still shut down the hall, the cold coffee still cold, Jessie's empty chair still empty, everything exactly as it had been an hour before except for the one thing that had just finished happening and would not need, from either of them, any further explanation.
