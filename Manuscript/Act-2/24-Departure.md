
# Chapter 24: Departure

*The farmhouse gate. The Alentejo. Morning.*

Branca had her bag packed before breakfast, which Sarah noticed and did not comment on, the same way she had noticed, the evening before, the particular economy with which Branca had folded the two shirts she'd brought and set them back exactly where they'd come from, as though a week were a thing that ought to leave no more trace in a room than it had to.

She had called Figueiredo herself the night before. Sarah found this out only when Branca mentioned it over coffee, plainly, the way she mentioned most things, not as a request for permission but as a fact already settled. "He'll come at half past eight," she said. "I hope that isn't too early."

"It isn't too early for anything." Sarah poured her own coffee and sat across from her, and neither of them said what they were both aware of, which was that a week was a strange unit of time to have held so much, and that its shortness was not the same as its being insufficient.

Jessie came in already dressed for school, Gerald tucked under one arm, and looked at Branca's bag by the door the way she looked at anything that told her something before anyone said it out loud. She did not ask why the bag was there. She had already worked that much out for herself two days ago, sitting across the table, and had filed the departure the same way she filed most things she could not do anything about: as a fact to be observed rather than argued with.

"You're going today," she said.

"I am."

Jessie considered this with her usual gravity, then climbed into her chair and reached for the bread, apparently satisfied that the matter required no further comment at breakfast. Some things, in Jessie's accounting, belonged to the gate, not the table. This, evidently, was one of them.

Daniel came in a few minutes later, and the four of them sat through an ordinary breakfast that was not quite ordinary, the small talk arriving a beat slower than usual, the silences carrying a little more weight than silences at that table usually carried. Nobody said the word goodbye. It sat in the room anyway, the way certain words do before anyone has agreed to use them.

At half past eight they stood at the gate.

The morning had the particular stillness of the Alentejo before the heat properly arrived, the light still soft, the courtyard wall not yet warm under Sarah's hand where she rested it without meaning to. Branca stood with her bag over one shoulder, the same canvas bag she had arrived with, looking somehow lighter than it had a week ago though it held, as far as Sarah knew, exactly the same things.

"I don't know how to do this part," Branca said. "I'm not good at leaving people who've done this for me."

"You don't have to be good at it," Sarah said. "You only have to do it."

Branca almost smiled at that, the same faint, unpracticed expression Sarah had seen once before, on the courtyard bench, gone again almost before it arrived. She looked at Jessie first, crouching a little to bring herself level, and Jessie regarded her with the same total attention she brought to everything she considered worth attending to.

"Thank you for showing me the courtyard," Branca said. "And Leonardo's stone. And Gerald's opinions on most matters."

"Gerald has a lot of opinions," Jessie said. "He'll probably have more about you leaving."

"I'd expect nothing less from Gerald."

Jessie nodded, satisfied, and did not offer her hand or ask for an embrace, and Branca did not reach for one either, and something in the way they simply looked at each other for a moment told Sarah that whatever passed between the two of them did not need translating into a gesture to be real.

Branca straightened and looked at Daniel next, then at Sarah, and something in her settled, the particular quiet of a person deciding to say the true thing rather than the easy one.

"I don't have the words for what this was," she said. "I've spent eleven years learning to sit with people who are trying to say something they don't have words for, and I still don't have them for my own turn at it. But I want to say this much, because it's true and because I think you'll understand it better than most people would." She looked down at her own hands for a moment, then back up. "I carry it with me now. The quality of what I received. It's in me. I don't think that's a metaphor. I think that's simply how it travels."

Sarah did not correct her, the way she had not corrected her in the kitchen. She only nodded, because there was nothing in the sentence that needed correcting, and because some things were true in exactly the plain way a person said them, without needing to be made more precise.

"Then it's already doing what it does," Sarah said.

Branca held that for a moment, and something passed over her face that was not quite a smile and not quite anything Sarah had a name for, and then the gravel sounded on the lane, unhurried, the particular crunch of Figueiredo taking the turn slowly out of courtesy, the way he always did.

He got out, lifted a hand in the same small economical wave he'd given a week before, and did not come through the gate. He understood, apparently, that this part belonged to the people standing in it and not to him.

Branca picked up her bag.

"Write to us," Daniel said. "If you want to. There's no obligation in it."

"I might." She looked at the farmhouse behind them, the courtyard wall, the fig tree, the low window of the room she'd slept in for a week. "I don't know yet what I'll want to say. But I know I won't forget how to find the address."

She went through the gate. Figueiredo held the passenger door, and she got in, and for a moment, through the windshield, Sarah could see her sitting very still, the same trained stillness she'd arrived with, though something in it, Sarah thought, had shifted its center of gravity, settled somewhere lower and steadier than it had been sitting the first evening.

The car pulled away down the lane, the sound of the engine fading the way it always faded, swallowed by the field and the distance and the particular quiet of a morning that had not yet decided how hot it meant to become.

Sarah closed the gate.

She stood there a moment with her hand still on the latch, Daniel beside her, Jessie already turning back toward the house with Gerald readjusted under her arm, already moving on to whatever came next in a six-year-old's morning, and Sarah understood, the way she had come to understand most of the important things in this life, late and all at once, that the farmhouse had just been returned to three people and that nothing about the return would look any different from the outside.

The courtyard was the courtyard. The fig tree was the fig tree. Somewhere below them, three stories down, the green lights burned in the dark exactly as they had burned every morning for two years, patient, unhurried, indifferent to the fact that something had just happened above them that they had, in their own way, already known.

What had changed could not be seen from the gate. Sarah stood there in the ordinary morning light, her hand still on the latch, and thought that the deepest changes in a life rarely came with a mark on them. They arrived, were received, and the world went on looking exactly as it had, indifferent to what had just moved through it.
