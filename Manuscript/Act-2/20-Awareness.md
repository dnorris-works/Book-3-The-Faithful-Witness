# Chapter 20: Awareness

*The farmhouse bedroom. The Alentejo. Night.*

They lay in the dark, close but not touching, settled into the particular ease two years of the same bed had taught them, the day draining out of both of them at its own pace.

"Jessie's teacher wants to talk to me about the multiplication tables," Sarah said. "Apparently she's decided seven times eight doesn't need to be memorized if you can derive it fast enough from other things you already know."

"Can she?"

"Faster than I can, some days."

Daniel laughed quietly into the dark, the kind of laugh that was mostly breath. "Gerald's going to want a position on this."

"Gerald has opinions about everything now. I think Gerald's portfolio has expanded past what the original bear was built to hold."

They went on like that a while, the ordinary currency of a day changing hands between them: the fig tree needing water sooner than expected, a note from Figueiredo about a request that could wait until next week, the particular quality of the light that afternoon, gold going to amber earlier than it had a month ago, the year tipping toward autumn a little at a time. Sarah felt Daniel's shoulder near hers, the warmth of him without the contact, and understood, as she had come to understand most things about this marriage, that this was itself a kind of conversation, this trading of small true things in the dark, even when neither of them was saying what the day had actually been about.

Daniel lay listening to the weather report of it, the fig tree, the teacher, the light, and underneath his own half of it he was aware of something else moving, a second conversation happening quietly beneath the one he was actually having. He had noticed it in himself since dinner: a carefulness in how he chose which small facts to offer, a sense that any of them, laid down wrong, might tip toward the larger thing neither of them had named yet. He was doing with Sarah exactly what he suspected she was doing with him. Circling. Choosing the safe true things first.

"She told me today that Gerald thinks the fig tree has feelings," Sarah said, of Jessie, and let it sit there a moment before adding, "not the leaves. The trunk, specifically. She was very precise about the distinction."

"What did you say?"

"I said I'd need more evidence before I could rule on it. She seemed satisfied with that." A pause. "She's not wrong to keep asking, though. I don't actually know where she got the certainty that trees might feel things. Not from me."

"From me, probably," Daniel said. "We've been arguing about it for a year. I think I'm losing."

"You are losing. She's got Gerald as a co-signatory now. You're outnumbered."

Daniel laughed again, quieter this time, and let the sound settle before he spoke. He had learned not to press when Sarah offered him something like that, only to receive it and let her know it had landed.

They were quiet again. Outside, faintly, the sound of the fig tree's leaves stirring in whatever small movement of night air had found its way into the courtyard, and further off, nothing, the particular deep quiet of the Alentejo after dark that neither of them had ever fully gotten used to, coming as they both had from cities where night never went fully silent.

Sarah was aware, lying there, of the room down the hall with its own light off now, its own particular quiet, a fourth person breathing somewhere in this house who had not been breathing here a week ago. She had not said Branca's name yet tonight. Neither had Daniel. They had talked about the fig tree and the teacher and the light instead, and she understood, somewhere under the talking, that this was not avoidance exactly. It was more like two people walking slowly toward the edge of a thing neither of them wanted to name before they were ready to be right about it.

Daniel felt the same pull and recognized it in the particular stillness that had come into Sarah's breathing, the stillness she got when she was holding something and deciding how long to hold it before setting it down. He had learned, across two years, that some silences of hers wanted company and some wanted room, and this one, he thought, wanted both at once, which was not a thing he had a method for, only an instinct.

He said it quietly, into the dark, because the dark made it easier to say a true thing without watching what your own face did while you said it.

"She's carrying something."

He did not say who. He did not need to.

Sarah did not answer right away. The sentence landed the same way MEMORIA's best answers landed, the ones that told her something she had already half known and had not yet said out loud to anyone, including herself. In the dark she nodded, and though he could not have seen it, she felt him register it anyway, some small shift in the quality of his stillness beside her that told her the nod had reached him regardless.

"I know," she said, finally, because the sentence deserved more than a nod even if the nod had been the truer answer.

Daniel did not ask her to say more, and she did not offer more, and neither of them reached for each other in the dark as they sometimes did when a day had been hard, because this was not that kind of night. This was a different kind of company, the kind that came from two people arriving at the same unfinished thought from two different directions and recognizing, without needing to compare notes, that they had landed in exactly the same place.

"We don't know what it is," Daniel said, after a while.

"No."

"Does that worry you?"

Sarah considered this honestly, turning it over as she'd turn an unresolved reading, refusing to force a conclusion the evidence hadn't earned yet. "Not how you'd think. I keep waiting for it to worry me and it doesn't arrive."

"Same," Daniel said.

They lay there a while longer, not speaking, the fig tree's leaves settling into stillness outside, the house holding its four sleeping and half-sleeping bodies in the dark exactly as it had held three for two years, adjusting itself around the new arithmetic without complaint. Neither of them said Branca's name before sleep came. It was not necessary. They had already said, between them, the only part of it that mattered tonight: that something was there, that they both saw it, and that they would carry the not-knowing together rather than each of them carrying it alone.
