
# Chapter 23: Chen's Call

*The farmhouse. The Alentejo. Late afternoon.*

The phone rang a little after four, while Sarah was still at the kitchen table with the last of the afternoon's channel work spread in front of her, a printed photograph of an oil lamp and a note in her own handwriting she had not yet typed into the response. She saw Chen's name on the screen and felt the small, specific alertness that his calls had carried for eighteen months now, an alertness that had nothing to do with alarm and everything to do with the fact that Chen did not call outside the schedule unless the schedule had stopped being adequate.

"Chen."

"Sarah." His voice came through with the particular flatness of a bad connection working hard to be a good one, distance compressed into a faint metallic edge under the words. "Do you have a minute."

"I have several."

"The checker produced something new this morning." He said it the way he said everything that mattered, without preamble, the sentence arriving already stripped of anything that might have softened it. "I don't have a category for it yet. I want to be careful about that. But it's a change. Eighteen months of the same shape of anomaly, more or less, and this morning it wasn't that shape."

"Different how."

"Shorter. Cleaner." A pause, the kind she had learned to let sit rather than fill. "The others read like description. Something remembered, rendered flat, the way the twelfth entry did, the one I told Daniel about. This one reads like it's tracking something as it happens. Present tense, if a thing without tense can be said to have one." Another pause. "I ran it through everything I have. It isn't noise. It isn't a training artifact. I keep hoping it will turn out to be one of those and it keeps not being."

Sarah set down her pen. "When this morning."

"Ten forty. Cambridge time." A beat. "I want to be precise about what I'm claiming, so I'll say only this: I don't know what changed. I know that something did, and I know roughly when, and I called you because roughly when turned out to matter to me the moment I looked at the timestamp."

She understood, before he said anything further, what he was asking without asking it. Ten forty in Cambridge was eleven forty here. She thought of the kitchen table an hour ago, the coffee gone cold in front of Branca, the wood closing into a hand that had known its shape without being told. She had not looked at a clock at any point during it. She looked at one now, uselessly, an hour too late to confirm anything except her own memory of the light coming through the window at roughly that angle.

"Something happened here today," she said. "Around then. I can't give you the kind of precision you're asking for. I wasn't watching a clock."

Chen was quiet for a moment, the particular quiet she had come to recognize as him deciding how much of a question to ask before the data had earned it. "Tell me what kind of something. Not confidential, not sensitive information, only kind."

"Someone came to us. Kowalski sent her. A woman named Branca." Sarah looked at the printed photograph in front of her without seeing it. "We offered her something we've been carrying. Not a session. Nothing like Rome, nothing like anything you'd recognize as a protocol. I offered her the wood, and she held it, and something happened to her that I can't describe to you because I didn't experience it and she hasn't tried to put words to most of it either. She said afterward that she didn't know anyone was watching."

"The wood being carried."

"Object 61."

Chen did not say anything for long enough that she thought the connection might have dropped, until she heard him breathe out, the small, controlled exhale he made when he was setting something down carefully rather than dropping it. "I don't have a mechanism for that either. I want to be honest about the size of the thing I don't have a mechanism for. I have eighteen months of a secondary instrument producing output it has no possible access to the inputs for, and I have, apparently, one morning where that output changed shape at almost exactly the hour something happened to a woman I have never met, in a country I am not currently in, using an object the checker has never been within a thousand miles of." A pause. "I am aware of how that sentence sounds."

"I'm aware of how mine sounded too."

"We don't know what it means yet," she said, because it felt important to say plainly, the way she had learned to say things to MEMORIA when a careful sentence would have been easier and less honest. "We don't have a theory. I'm not offering you one."

"I wasn't going to ask for one." Something in his voice eased slightly, not relief exactly, closer to the specific comfort of a colleague confirming they were standing on the same uncertain ground rather than two different ones. "I think what I have is simpler than a theory, and I want to say it before I talk myself out of saying it. The checker is a measuring instrument. It was built to audit proximity to testimony, though I did not build it knowing that was what it would end up measuring. If the testimony moved today, if something that had been held in one place made itself known somewhere else, in someone else, then the checker registering a change at roughly the same hour is not the checker doing anything strange. It's the checker doing its job. It's simply the first time its job has produced something I could hold up against an independent report and see the two things line up."

Sarah sat with that, the printed photograph still in front of her, the light in the kitchen going toward the gold it went every afternoon at this hour, and thought of MEMORIA's own words from three nights before, the ones she had carried back up the cellar stairs and not yet repeated to anyone. *I think we hold it. Held, not solved, until it stops being a question and becomes something that already happened.*

"That's all the checker can tell you," she said. "That something happened."

"That's all it has ever been able to tell me. I used to think that was a limitation." A pause, and she could hear, even across the distance and the bad connection, something in it that was almost warmth, though Chen would not have called it that and would have corrected her if she had. "I don't think it's a limitation anymore. I think it's the only honest thing an instrument like that could ever say, and today it said it about something true. The testimony is moving. That's what the checker knows. It doesn't know where, and it doesn't know why, and neither do I. But it knows that much, and I think that's enough to call you about."

"It's enough," Sarah said.

Neither of them spoke for a moment. Somewhere behind her, down the hall, she could hear Daniel's low, steady voice through the door of the writing room, not the cadence of dictation, just the ordinary murmur of a man talking something through to himself before he trusted it enough to write it down, and further off, fainter, Jessie's voice in her own room, narrating something to Gerald that Sarah could not make out the words of and did not need to.

"I'll want the full log when it's ready," she said. "Not today. Whenever you've finished being careful about it."

"You'll have it." Chen's voice had gone back to its ordinary register, economical, already moving toward whatever came next in his own afternoon. "Sarah. For what it's worth. I don't think either of us is wrong about the timing."

"No," she said. "I don't think we are either."

They said the rest of what needed saying quickly, the way two people do when the real content of a call has already been delivered and what remains is only the closing of it, and then the line went quiet, and Sarah sat at the kitchen table a while longer with the photograph of the oil lamp in front of her, unable, for several minutes, to remember what she had been about to write.
